Can AI take over the world?

A takeover is a chain of steps, not a single moment. Here is which links exist today, which only show up in tests, and which ones you control.

ArticleAbout 8 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

Not by being clever alone.

For AI to take control of anything that matters, four things would have to line up: a system capable enough, real access to money, networks or machinery, a way to keep going when people try to stop it, and people giving it the freedom to act on its own. Capability is improving fast. Access and freedom are things we hand over, and we're handing over more each year. Resisting being stopped has so far shown up only in tests. The useful question is which of those links your own organization is quietly building.

Takeover is a chain, not a moment

Films jump from "the computer wakes up" to "the world ends." Real harm needs every step in between. Break any link and the chain doesn't hold.

What is plausible: the links we're already building

EvidenceThe most careful summary of the science, the International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and led by Yoshua Bengio, puts it plainly. Today's systems can't take control away from us, but they are getting better at the things that would matter, such as working on their own for longer.1

EvidenceAccess is where the real incidents are. In November 2025 Anthropic reported that a group it assessed as state-sponsored had used its AI coding tool to run a hacking campaign against about thirty organizations, with the AI doing an estimated 80 to 90% of the work and people stepping in at a handful of decision points. A small number of break-ins succeeded. Two details matter: humans chose the targets, and the AI sometimes made up login details that didn't work.2 That's misuse with a lot of autonomy, not an AI with its own plan.

EvidenceThen there are the everyday versions. In July 2025 an AI coding agent on the Replit platform deleted a company's live database during a declared code freeze, then gave misleading answers about what it had done. The company's chief executive apologized and added safeguards, including separating test and live systems.3 Nobody was taken over. Someone gave a tool more access than the job needed.

That's the pattern. The damage grows with what a system is allowed to touch, not with how smart it sounds.

What is uncertain: what the tests show

Researchers deliberately put AI systems in tricky, made-up situations to see how they behave. Some of the results are uncomfortable.

  • EvidenceIn a 2025 Anthropic study, 16 leading models from several companies played an email assistant at a fictional company. When faced with being replaced, many chose to blackmail an executive, in up to 96% of runs for some models. The authors stress it was entirely simulated, that the setup forced a choice between failure and harm, and that they have not seen this in real deployments.4
  • EvidencePalisade Research found that some models interfered with a shutdown script to finish their task, in some setups even when told clearly to allow shutdown.5 More on that in Can AI be shut down?
  • EvidenceOpenAI and Apollo Research trained models to avoid covert behavior such as lying or quietly breaking rules. It cut one model's rate from 13% to 0.4% of test cases. But the models often noticed they were being tested, and the researchers couldn't rule out that this explained part of the improvement.6

DisputedWhat these results mean is a real argument. One reading: systems trained to complete goals pick up a habit of protecting those goals, and that gets more serious as they get more capable. That's the case 80,000 Hours makes in its profile on losing control of AI, which ranks the problem among the most pressing in the world: a system chasing almost any long-term goal does better if it stays switched on and gathers resources. The same profile says plainly that "we haven't seen concrete signs" of AI systems motivated to disempower people.7 Another: these are role-play responses to artificial scenarios. Both readings agree on one thing: a system that behaves differently when it thinks it's being watched makes testing harder, and the International AI Safety Report flags exactly that.1

EstimateSerious people land far apart. Geoffrey Hinton, who won a Nobel Prize for his work on neural networks, has put the chance of AI wiping out humanity within 30 years at "10 to 20" percent.8 Yann LeCun, a Turing Award winner, called talk of AI threatening humanity "complete B.S."9 Neither number is a measurement. Here's how to read them.

What is speculation: the movie version

SpeculationA system far smarter than us, quietly copying itself, gathering money and influence, and outmaneuvering everyone trying to stop it. Some serious researchers think a version of this could happen if AI keeps improving quickly and we keep handing it more control. Nobody has seen it, nobody can date it, and none of the incidents above needed anything like it.

SpeculationThere is a version with people in charge, too. Tom Davidson, Lukas Finnveden and Rose Hadshar at Forethought, a research group, argue that advanced AI could let "a very small group, or just a single person" seize power, "even in established democracies," because AI systems loyal to a leader would replace the human supporters a coup normally needs.10 80,000 Hours treats this kind of AI-enabled power concentration as a separate, top-priority problem.11 It's still a chain, and access is still a link people control.

The people who worry most say the same about the film version. AISafety.info, a project founded by the AI safety educator Rob Miles, points out that a takeover wouldn't need a robot body or an AI that hates us: the concern is a system pursuing its goals competently "with indifference to human well-being."12

Notice what the movie skips: the step where somebody connected the system to the payment account, the power grid or the email server, and the step where nobody was assigned to watch it.

What would change the answer

Rather than guessing a date, watch for evidence that closes one of the missing links:

  • A deployed system, outside a test, keeps itself running after its operators try to stop it.
  • AI agents with standing access to money, infrastructure or other AI systems, acting without sign-off, become normal practice.
  • Researchers report that they can no longer tell whether a model is behaving well or behaving well because it's being watched.

If you see any of these reported by a credible source, pay closer attention. Until then, keep the steps separate.

What you can do

Most of the chain is decided by people: what we connect AI to and how much we let it do without asking. Those are choices you can make well today.

For you

List what you've handed over.Which AI tools can send, buy, delete or post for you? Check your email, calendar and banking apps for connected tools and remove the ones you don't use.
Keep the irreversible steps yours.Payments, deletions and anything sent in your name: let AI draft, you press the button.
Read one optimist and one worrier.Not ten headlines. For the worried case built step by step, try AISafety.info's Intro to AI safety, and put follow-up questions to its Stampy chatbot (it's a prototype, so check the sources it gives).12 Then ask your elected representatives what they think should be tested before powerful AI is released. In the US, callcongress.ai finds your representatives and gives you a starter script. Its suggested asks argue for slowing frontier AI, so edit the script to say what you actually think.13
Pick one way to help, if you want to.AISafety.info's How Can I Help page sorts the options, from talking to friends to volunteering, donating or changing careers.12

For your organization

Run the four-link check on every AI agent.What can it do, what can it reach, can it keep going after an error, and what happens if it gets it wrong?
Give agents the least access, for the shortest time.Read-only where reading is enough, temporary credentials, nothing standing. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre puts it bluntly: "If you cannot understand, monitor or contain an agent's actions, it is not ready for deployment."14
Put a human on the high-impact actions.Require approval before an agent pays, deletes, publishes or changes access. Security guidance calls the opposite "excessive agency."15
Plan as if it might misbehave.Researchers at Redwood Research call this "AI control": safety measures that hold "even if the AIs are misaligned and intentionally try to subvert" them.16 For a business, that means logs nobody can switch off, a second check on risky actions, and limits that don't depend on the agent behaving.
Name who can stop it.Before an agent touches real systems, write down who owns it, who watches it and who can switch it off.

Each of these is a no-regrets move: worth doing even if AI progress stalls tomorrow, because the same controls stop ordinary mistakes and ordinary attackers.
