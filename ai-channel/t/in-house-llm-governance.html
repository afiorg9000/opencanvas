We're building our own AI. What do we need to govern?

For organizations that build on, fine-tune or host AI models themselves. Seven jobs a vendor used to do for you, how to do each one, and how they map to NIST, ISO/IEC 42001 and the EU AI Act.

About 14 minutesLast checked November 2026Printable checklist at the end

The short answer

When you run the model, you inherit the jobs a vendor's contract used to cover: security, privacy, testing, logging, human oversight and incident response. Plus one more that's always yours: someone accountable for all of it.

You don't need to invent a framework. Use the NIST AI Risk Management Framework for structure, ISO/IEC 42001 if you want something you can certify against, and the EU AI Act to work out your legal role if you operate in Europe. Then scale the effort to what you're actually doing, because a chatbot over your policy documents isn't a model that screens job applicants.

First, what are you actually building?

"In-house AI" covers three very different things. Governance should scale with the level, so place yourself first.

LevelWhat it looks likeWhat's new for you
1 · Build on a modelAn assistant that answers from your documents, using a provider's model through an API (often called retrieval, or RAG)Access to your documents, prompt injection, logging, output checks
2 · Fine-tune a modelYou train an existing model further on your own data so it writes or classifies your wayAll of level 1, plus training-data privacy, model versioning and re-testing after every change
3 · Host a modelYou run an open-weight model on your own servers or cloud accountAll of the above, plus infrastructure security, model provenance, patching and capacity

Separately from the level, ask what the system is used for. A model that helps decide on jobs, credit, benefits or access to services carries far more weight than one that drafts newsletters, whatever level it sits at.

1. Ownership: one name per system

Every system needs one accountable owner, a business person rather than only an engineer, who signs off on its purpose, its risks and its release. Keep an inventory: what the system is, which model and version it uses, what data it touches, who uses it and what it's allowed to do.

This is what NIST calls the "Govern" function, which runs across the other three (Map, Measure, Manage).1 ISO/IEC 42001 asks for the same thing as a management system: policy, roles, risk assessment and continual improvement.3

2. Security: assume the model can be talked into things

Language models can't reliably tell your instructions apart from instructions hidden inside the content they read. That's prompt injection, and it's number one on the OWASP list of risks for AI applications.7 The UK National Cyber Security Centre says it "may never be totally mitigated" and advises designing so a fooled model can't do much harm.8

  • Least privilege. A model that reads untrusted content (email, web pages, uploaded files) shouldn't also hold powerful tools. Give each tool the narrowest permission that works. OWASP calls the opposite "excessive agency."7
  • Deterministic guards. Put ordinary code, not another prompt, between the model and anything that moves money, sends messages or changes records. Deny-lists of bad phrases don't hold up; attackers rephrase.8
  • No secrets in prompts. System prompts leak (OWASP lists "system prompt leakage" as its own risk). Keep API keys and passwords in a secrets manager, never in a prompt or a document the model can retrieve.7
  • Access control on the model and its data. Role-based access and multi-factor sign-in for the model, its admin console and its document store. In IBM's 2025 breach study, 97% of organizations that had an AI-related breach said they lacked proper AI access controls.9
  • Know where the model came from. Download models and libraries from sources you can verify, record versions, and treat model files like any other third-party code (OWASP: supply chain, and data and model poisoning).7

For a fuller checklist, the UK and US cyber agencies' Guidelines for secure AI system development and the UK's AI Cyber Security Code of Practice (January 2025) cover design, development, deployment and operation.1213 MITRE ATLAS catalogs real attack techniques against AI systems if your security team wants to threat-model.14

3. Privacy: models can remember

If you fine-tune on personal data, some of it can come back out. Researchers showed in 2021 that they could extract verbatim training text, including names and contact details, from a public language model by querying it.10 Europe's data protection board concluded in December 2024 that a model trained on personal data can't automatically be treated as anonymous; it has to be very unlikely that anyone could pull that data out through queries.11

  • Don't train on what you don't need. Strip or pseudonymize personal data before fine-tuning. Often retrieval (level 1) does the job without putting personal data into the model's weights at all.
  • Respect permissions in retrieval. An assistant over your shared drive must only show each user what that user is already allowed to open. Otherwise it becomes the fastest way to read the HR folder. OWASP lists this under vector and embedding weaknesses.7
  • Do the impact assessment. If the system processes personal data in a way that's likely to be high-risk, data protection law will usually require a data protection impact assessment before you start.

If you're still choosing between building and buying, The AI Sandbox compares private models with the other ways to keep data under control.

4. Evaluation: test it like you mean it

Generic benchmark scores tell you little about your use. Build your own test set from real tasks, with known good answers, and include the awkward cases: the angry customer, the question it should refuse, the document with a hidden instruction in it.

  • Test before release and after every change. A new model version, a new system prompt or a new data source can all change behavior. Re-run the same tests and compare.
  • Red-team it. Have people who didn't build it try to make it leak, misbehave or give harmful advice.
  • Measure what matters. Accuracy on your tasks, rate of made-up facts, refusals, bias across groups where the system affects people, and cost per task.

NIST's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) lists suggested actions for testing generative systems, organized by the four functions.2

5. Logging: if it isn't written down, it didn't happen

Record enough to reconstruct any answer: the user, the input, what was retrieved, which tools were called, the output, and the model and prompt version. The NCSC recommends logging inputs, outputs and tool calls so you can spot attacks.8

Two cautions. Logs of AI conversations are themselves sensitive data, so restrict who can read them and set a retention period. And if a system counts as high-risk under the EU AI Act, deployers must keep its automatically generated logs for at least six months (Article 26), and providers must build in automatic logging (Article 12).4

6. Human oversight: a real person with a real stop button

"A human reviews it" only counts if that person has the time, training and authority to disagree. The EU AI Act spells this out for high-risk systems (Article 14): the people overseeing must understand the system's limits, stay aware of "automation bias" (the habit of trusting the machine), be able to override the output, and be able to halt the system with a "stop" button or similar.4 That's a good standard for any consequential system, high-risk or not.

  • Decide in advance which outputs a person must approve, and which can go straight through.
  • Watch the override rate. If reviewers never disagree, they may have stopped looking.

7. Incident response: plan for the bad day

Decide what counts as an AI incident before you have one: a data leak through the model, harmful or discriminatory output, a successful prompt injection, an agent taking an action it shouldn't.

  • Have a switch. Know how to turn the system off, or roll back to the last tested model and prompt, within minutes.
  • Fold it into your existing process. Same on-call, same severity levels, same post-incident review as any other system.
  • Know your reporting duties. Data breaches have their own deadlines under data protection law. Under the EU AI Act, providers of high-risk systems must report serious incidents to the authorities, at the latest 15 days after becoming aware (Article 73).4

How the frameworks fit

They aren't competitors. NIST gives you the structure and a playbook of actions; it's voluntary and free, and NIST says it's being revised.1 ISO/IEC 42001 (December 2023) is a management-system standard you can be independently certified against, which matters if customers ask for proof.3 The EU AI Act is law, with obligations that depend on your role and the system's risk tier.

The EU AI Act: which role are you?

The Act applies if you're in the EU, and also if you're outside it but the system's output is used in the EU.4 Your duties depend on whether you're a provider (you develop the system and put it on the market or into service under your name) or a deployer (you use it under your authority).

  • Building on a model for your own use usually makes you the provider of that AI system and its deployer. If the use is high-risk (for example hiring, credit or access to essential services), the heavy obligations apply.
  • Changing someone else's system can make you its provider. Under Article 25, you take on provider duties if you substantially modify a high-risk system, or change a system's purpose so that it becomes high-risk.4
  • Fine-tuning a general-purpose model rarely makes you a provider of that model. The Commission's guidelines use an indicative test: you become one if your modification uses more than a third of the compute originally used to train it.6 Most business fine-tuning is far below that.

The dates, after the 2026 changes

The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. A simplification package, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was published in July 2026 and moved some dates.5

FromWhat applies
2 Feb 2025Banned practices; AI literacy. The 2026 amendment reworded literacy as a duty to take measures to support staff AI literacy.
2 Aug 2025Obligations for providers of general-purpose AI models
2 Aug 2026Most remaining provisions, including transparency duties (Article 50) such as telling people they're dealing with AI
2 Dec 2026New bans added in 2026 (including AI that generates non-consensual intimate images); deadline to label AI-generated content for generative systems already on the market
2 Dec 2027High-risk rules for stand-alone systems in Annex III areas, such as employment, credit and education (was 2 Aug 2026)
2 Aug 2028High-risk rules for AI built into regulated products under Annex I (was 2 Aug 2027)
Deferred, not dropped. The high-risk obligations still come. Systems you design now will be running when they apply, so build the logs, tests and oversight in from the start. Fines for breaching most obligations reach EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; for banned practices, EUR 35 million or 7%.4 Not sure which tier you're in? Starter checklist

For the team building it

  1. Put one name on it. An accountable owner for each system, written in the inventory with its model, version, data and purpose.
  2. Draw the blast radius. List every tool, document store and action the model can reach. Cut anything it doesn't need.
  3. Move secrets out of prompts. Keys and passwords in a secrets manager. Assume the system prompt will be read by a user one day.
  4. Check retrieval permissions. Log in as an ordinary user and try to retrieve something you shouldn't see.
  5. Build the 50-question test set. Real tasks, known answers, awkward cases. Run it before every release.
  6. Log the full trail. User, input, retrieved content, tool calls, output, model and prompt version. Restrict who can read it.
  7. Name the reviewer and the stop button. Who approves what, and how to switch it off or roll back in minutes.
  8. Write the incident page. What counts as an AI incident, who's called, and which reporting clocks start.

For the leadership team

  1. Ask for the inventory. If nobody can list your in-house AI systems and their owners in a day, start there.
  2. Pick your framework on purpose. NIST for structure; ISO/IEC 42001 if customers or regulators will want certification; the EU AI Act if you operate in or sell into Europe.
