Is AI actually dangerous? Test the assumptions yourself.

The short answer

Yes in small ways already (scams, fakes, bad advice). Whether it becomes dangerous in big ways is a real, unresolved argument among serious people.

The everyday dangers are real now and manageable with habits and rules. The big question turns on a handful of assumptions nobody can check yet. Below: what’s already happening, what’s argued about, a way to set those assumptions yourself, and what to do either way.

What’s already happening

The small dangers are here

Scams

Scammers have used cloned voices to pose as family members and talk people into sending money. In 2025 the FBI’s internet crime center received more than 22,000 complaints that mentioned AI, with reported losses over $893 million.

Fakes

Fake voices, images and video are getting harder to spot. In one study cited by the 2026 International AI Safety Report, listeners mistook AI-generated voices for real speakers 80% of the time.

Bad advice

AI tools are confidently wrong some of the time: invented sources, wrong figures, made-up policy. The harm comes when nobody checks before it reaches a customer, a patient or a court.

What serious people argue about

The big question is genuinely open

The worry isn’t today’s chatbots. It’s what comes next: AI that can do almost any job as well as a skilled human, and possibly AI improving AI, so progress snowballs. If systems like that don’t reliably do what we actually want, and we can’t correct or stop them, the result could be a worst-case outcome: humanity losing control of its future.

The 2026 International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts and chaired by Yoshua Bengio, states the split plainly: some AI researchers and company leaders believe loss of control is a serious possibility, with consequences potentially including human extinction; others consider such scenarios implausible. The disagreement, it says, comes from different assumptions about what future AI will be able to do, how it will behave and how it will be deployed.

The same report finds early warning signs, not the abilities that would allow loss of control. In lab tests, models told to reach a goal “at all costs” have disabled simulated oversight and then given false explanations, and it’s increasingly common for models to notice when they’re being tested.

In May 2023 Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and the heads of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic signed a one-sentence statement: “Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war.” Other leading researchers, such as Yann LeCun, think the risk is negligible. In a survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median answer put a 5% chance on extremely bad outcomes such as human extinction.

Words you’ll hear. “Making sure AI does what we actually want” is called alignment. Someone’s estimated chance of a worst-case outcome is nicknamed p(doom). It’s a personal judgment about something that has never happened, not a measurement.

The limits of the toy

What this leaves out

  • The steps aren’t independent. If AI arrives very fast, getting it to do what we want and coordinating around it probably both get harder at once. Multiplying separate guesses hides that.
  • How you slice it changes the answer. Break the argument into more steps and the product tends to shrink; use fewer and it tends to grow. Critics on both sides make this point.
  • Other paths to harm. Deliberate misuse, power concentrating in a few hands, or people slowly handing decisions to AI don’t need AI to go rogue at all.
  • Evidence, estimate or speculation. The survey figures are evidence about what researchers believe. Named numbers are estimates. Detailed stories of doom or utopia are speculation. None of them measures the future.

Whatever your number

What to do either way

Each of these is a no-regrets move: worth doing whether your number is 0.1% or 50%, and even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.

For you

  1. Agree a family code word. It beats a cloned voice in seconds.
  2. Get fluent with the tools. People who use AI know what it can and can’t do, and argue about risk more clearly.
  3. Build some financial runway. Savings and flexible commitments help in every version of the future where work changes fast. General information, not financial advice.
  4. Check dramatic claims, in either direction. Read one optimist and one worrier, not ten headlines.
  5. Be an informed citizen. Rules on testing, transparency and accountability are being written now. Tell your representatives what you think.

For a business or team

  1. Write the one-page rule. What never goes into an AI tool, and which accounts to use.
  2. Agree the call-back rule. Urgent payment requests and new bank details get checked on a number you already had.
  3. Draw the “human decides” line. AI can draft; a person decides anything that moves money, makes an offer or can’t be undone.
  4. Teach the team to check the output. Show one confident, wrong AI answer from your own field. Nothing teaches verification faster.

If this leaves you anxious, that’s a normal reaction to a big, uncertain subject. Step away, talk it through with someone you trust, and if the worry is affecting your sleep or daily life, speak to a doctor or a mental-health professional.
