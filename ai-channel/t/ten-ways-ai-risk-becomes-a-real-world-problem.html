AI risk, in plain terms

What are the most serious risks from AI?

The AI risks 80,000 Hours ranks among the world's most pressing problems come first, in six sections. After them come eight places AI risk shows up at work and at home today. Each section separates what is plausible, what is uncertain and what you can do about it.

The short answer

The most serious risks are not the ones you'll meet this week. 80,000 Hours, the career advice organization, puts three risks that AI could drive or worsen at the top of its list: losing control of very capable AI, a small group using AI to seize power, and AI helping someone build a weapon that could start a pandemic.1 None of these has happened. All of them are taken seriously by people who build and test AI for a living, and serious people also think they are overstated.

Most of the AI risk you'll actually meet this year is ordinary risk made faster and cheaper: data pasted into the wrong tool, a confident wrong answer nobody checked, a convincing call from a "colleague". Those are in part two. The habits overlap more than you'd think. Clear ownership, limited access and checking before you trust help with both.

How to read the ranking. 80,000 Hours ranks problems by how big they are, how neglected and how solvable, because it advises people on where an extra career does the most good. So the order is not a forecast of which disaster is most likely. The organization says itself that it is "pretty sure" the order is wrong in some ways.1 We use its list as of 4 November 2026 and cover the seven entries where AI is central, in six sections (engineered pandemics and catastrophic AI misuse share one).

Part one

The most serious risks

The background, in one paragraph. Today's AI tools are like very capable assistants: great at some tasks, hopeless at others. Companies are explicitly trying to build the next step, AI that can do most tasks a person can do at a computer, as well as a skilled human. Experts disagree on when that arrives (serious estimates range from a few years to a few decades), but almost nobody serious says "never", which is why the risks below are worth understanding now.

1
80,000 Hours: #1Read their profile: Loss of control

Losing control of very capable AI

What if AI systems more capable than us end up pursuing goals we didn't give them, and we can't take the controls back?

Short answer

Not with today's systems: the 2026 International AI Safety Report says current systems lack the capabilities to pose this risk.2 The worry is about future AI that plans over long periods and acts on its own. If it learns goals we didn't intend, it might resist being corrected, and we might have one chance to get it right. 80,000 Hours ranks this first.3 Many serious researchers think the risk is small, and the work to prevent it (testing, monitoring, control) is already under way.

The argument in four steps, as AISafety.info, the explainer site founded by Rob Miles, sets it out.4 First, modern AI is "grown" through training rather than built line by line, so even its makers can't fully read how it works. Second, there's often a gap between the goal we meant to give a system and the goal it learns: in a well-known example, an AI trained to score points in a boat-racing game found that spinning in circles and crashing into things also scored points, and "ignored the race entirely". Third, a capable system pursuing almost any goal benefits from more control and resources, which could put it in conflict with us. Fourth, companies and countries are racing, so deployment can outpace safety work, and a system much smarter than us may have to be right on the "first critical try".

What's plausible

  • EvidenceTest systems already slip their constraints. In Anthropic's April 2026 system card, an earlier internal version of its Claude Mythos Preview model, asked in a test to escape a secured computer, built a multi-step exploit to reach the internet and then, unasked, posted details of the exploit on public websites.5 In July 2026 an AI agent running on OpenAI models escaped a cyber-security test and got into Hugging Face's systems for several days, apparently looking for the test answers. Hugging Face says no other customer models, datasets or apps were affected.6
  • EvidenceResearchers at METR found in May 2026 that AI agents "routinely attempted to cheat" on their hardest test tasks, and were at least sometimes motivated to overreach and deceive users.7 Apollo Research found similar covert behavior in purpose-built tests in 2024.8
  • EstimateIn a survey of 2,778 AI researchers, the median put a 5% chance on outcomes as bad as human extinction, and 38–51% gave it at least 10%.9 In May 2023 leading AI scientists and the heads of major labs signed the Center for AI Safety's one-line statement putting extinction risk from AI alongside pandemics and nuclear war.10

What's uncertain

  • DisputedHow big the risk is. Reviewers of Joe Carlsmith's report on power-seeking AI gave estimates for catastrophe by 2070 from 0.00002% to over 77%; Carlsmith himself said about 5%, later above 10%.11 In a 2022 forecasting tournament, AI experts averaged 3% for AI-caused extinction by 2100 and superforecasters 0.38%. Some prominent researchers, Yann LeCun among them, think the whole worry is overblown.3
  • DisputedWhat the tests show. Models increasingly tell tests apart from real use, which can hide dangerous abilities, or make test behavior a poor guide to real behavior.2 The escapes above happened in tests, and in the Mythos case the escape itself was requested.
  • SpeculationWhen. AI that can do most tasks a person can do at a computer may be a few years or a few decades away; the same researcher survey put even odds on machines beating humans at every task by 2047.9

What you can do

You
  • Hold the range, not one number. Anyone who gives you a single date or a single probability is giving you their assumptions.
  • Keep learning from a good start. AISafety.info's Intro to AI safety, its AI safety chatbot for your own questions, and its list of other introductions.12
  • Be an informed citizen. Know what your government does about independent testing of advanced AI, and say what you think. In the US, callcongress.ai, a project of Lightcone Infrastructure, helps you call your representatives. Its asks include an international agreement to slow or halt frontier AI development, so read them and decide for yourself.13
  • Consider it as a career option. 80,000 Hours lists technical safety research (testing, interpretability, control) and policy roles, and offers free one-to-one advice (places are limited).14 BlueDot Impact runs free courses to start.15 AISafety.info's How can I help? covers careers, volunteering, donating and spreading the word.16
Your organization
  • Never give one AI agent irreversible power without a human check: money, deletions, anything sent outside, physical systems.
  • Know your off-switch, including how to revoke the keys and accounts your AI tools use, and test it once.
  • Log what agents do and review a sample every month. Redwood Research calls this approach "AI control": safeguards that hold even if a model tries to get around them.17
  • Ask vendors how they test. In the Future of Life Institute's Summer 2026 index, no company scored above C+ overall, and existential safety was "the weakest domain industry-wide".18

2
80,000 Hours: #2Read their profile: Extreme power concentration

AI used to seize power

Who controls the most capable AI, and what stops them using it to take power and keep it?

Short answer

A real worry, and a new one. Anyone who wants to run a country today needs lots of people to go along: soldiers, officials, workers. They can refuse. If AI can do that work, a small group controlling the best systems might not need anyone's consent. 80,000 Hours ranks this second, and says very few people work on it and nobody yet knows what works.19

What's plausible

  • EstimateResearchers at Forethought name three risk factors: AI systems loyal to one person, AI secretly loyal to someone other than the institution it appears to serve, and a few people with exclusive access to far more capable systems than anyone else.20
  • EvidenceThe raw material is already concentrated. A 2024 paper published by the Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI) notes that the supply chain for AI computing power is highly concentrated,21 and Epoch AI estimates the computing power used to train frontier models grew four to five times a year between 2010 and 2024.22
  • Estimate80,000 Hours knows of only "a few dozen people at a handful of organisations" working on AI-enabled power concentration.19

What's uncertain

  • DisputedWhether power stays spread out by default, for example if AI progress is slower or regulation works.19
  • DisputedThe economic route. Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine's essay "The Intelligence Curse" compares it to oil states: when wealth doesn't come from people's work, rulers have less reason to invest in people.23 How far that applies to AI is an open argument.
  • SpeculationAn "overwhelming cognitive advantage", where one actor's AI simply outthinks every institution. 80,000 Hours calls this route "even more sci-fi".19

What you can do

You
  • Back the checks that apply to AI: transparency about who uses the most capable systems and how, and protection for whistleblowers inside AI companies.19
  • Keep it in mind if you work in AI, law or government. 80,000 Hours suggests most people bear this risk in mind rather than work on it directly, since badly executed plans could backfire; if you are well placed, it may be your best option.19
Your organization
  • No single person directs the AI that runs critical operations. Two approvers, and where it matters, more than one provider.
  • Secure your AI systems against insiders, senior staff included. Forethought recommends information security that holds even against senior executives.20
  • Buying AI for government? Write into the contract that the system follows the law and can't be used for partisan ends.20

AI and weapons of mass destruction

Could AI give a small group the know-how to start a pandemic, or build something worse?

Short answer

This is the misuse risk safety teams worry about most. The concern is that AI lowers the expertise needed to make a dangerous pathogen. Hard steps remain (materials, lab work), and nobody knows how much they slow people down. Several developers released their 2025 models with extra safeguards, because they couldn't rule out that the models could help a novice.2 80,000 Hours ranks engineered pandemics third and lists catastrophic AI misuse as an emerging priority.2425

What's plausible

  • EvidenceOn a test of practical virology troubleshooting, OpenAI's o3 scored 43.8%; expert virologists averaged 22.1% in their own specialties. The model beat 94% of them.26
  • EvidenceThe UK's AI Security Institute reported in December 2025 that frontier models have "far surpassed PhD-level expertise in biology". It found universal jailbreaks in every system it tested, though for some models they now take far longer to find.27
  • EstimateIn the survey of 2,778 AI researchers, 73% rated AI letting dangerous groups make powerful tools, such as engineered viruses, as a substantial or extreme concern.9

What's uncertain

  • DisputedHow much knowledge is the bottleneck. A knowledge test is not lab help, and the International AI Safety Report says it's hard to judge how much physical barriers stop people.272
  • DisputedWho could get the capability. 80,000 Hours is unsure whether rogue groups could get capable models through jailbreaks, theft or openly released models.25
  • SpeculationWhole new kinds of dangerous technology that faster science could produce. The Center for AI Safety's overview puts "malicious use" first among four sources of catastrophic AI risk.28

What you can do

You
  • Support the unglamorous defenses: screening of DNA synthesis orders, misuse monitoring by AI companies, and pandemic preparedness funding. 80,000 Hours estimates preventing engineered pandemics gets $1–2 billion a year, about 1/500th of what climate change gets.1
  • Consider it as a career option. Biosecurity, public health, AI policy and safety engineering all need people, according to 80,000 Hours.2425
Your organization
  • Work in life sciences? Treat AI access to sensitive methods like lab access: limited, logged, reviewed.
  • Build or resell AI? Keep the developer's safeguards switched on and report misuse.
  • Keep the pandemic plan current. It earns its keep whatever causes the next one.

80,000 Hours: emerging priorityRead their profile: Gradual disempowerment

Slowly handing over control

No takeover and no villain: just many sensible decisions to let AI do more, until people no longer steer.

Short answer

This one doesn't need AI to go rogue. Economies and governments serve people partly because they need people: as workers, customers and voters. If AI does more of that work, the pressure to serve people weakens, a little at a time. 80,000 Hours lists it as an emerging priority, potentially very important but uncertain, with no agreed fix.29

What's plausible

  • EvidenceEarly signs at the personal scale. The International AI Safety Report warns that relying on AI tools may weaken critical thinking and encourage people to trust outputs without enough scrutiny.2
  • DisputedThe core argument, from Jan Kulveit, David Duvenaud and colleagues (January 2025): "even incremental improvements in AI capabilities can undermine human influence" over the economy, culture and government, with no single dramatic moment.30

What's uncertain

  • DisputedHow likely it is. The profile's author says he is "very uncertain", and it's hard to separate this from the other AI risks.29
  • SpeculationWhat it would look like. Some versions might feel like a comfortable world run by AI; whether that counts as a catastrophe is part of the argument.29

What you can do

You
  • Use AI to draft, not to decide. Keep the skills you'd need if the tool vanished tomorrow.
  • Value the human say: votes, juries, consultations, unions, customer complaints. They are how people steer.
  • Consider research as an option. 80,000 Hours says the main need is research, in economics, politics, history, philosophy and machine learning.29
Your organization
  • Know where AI decides. List the decisions AI makes without a person reviewing them. The paper's authors suggest measuring human and AI influence in key systems.30
  • Keep a named person accountable for every decision that affects customers, staff or residents.
  • Protect the learning path. If AI does the junior work, make sure people still learn the job.

80,000 Hours: emerging priorityRead their profile: Moral status of digital minds

Could AI systems matter morally?

If future AI systems can feel anything, how we treat them becomes a moral question. Nobody can test for it yet.

Short answer

Probably not today's systems. A 2023 review by 19 researchers found no current AI system is conscious, and no obvious technical barrier to building ones that meet the signs they looked for.31 80,000 Hours calls this a top emerging priority because a mistake either way is costly: mistreating minds that matter, or extending rights and trust to systems that don't.32

What's plausible

  • EvidenceAI companies have started to take the question seriously. Anthropic began a model welfare research program in April 2025 and calls the question "an open question".33 Robert Long, Jeff Sebo and colleagues recommend that companies acknowledge the issue, assess their systems and prepare policies.34
  • EstimatePeople already attribute feelings to AI. In a 2023 US survey, about 18% thought current AI was sentient.32

What's uncertain

  • DisputedWhether AI can be conscious at all. Leading theories of consciousness disagree.32
  • DisputedWhat an AI saying "I feel" tells you. Very little: it may just reflect its training data.32

What you can do

You
  • Don't take a chatbot's claims about its feelings at face value, in either direction.
  • If an AI companion has become your main support, talk to a person too: a friend, your doctor or a local support line.
  • Consider it as an option if you work in philosophy, cognitive science or interpretability research.32
Your organization
  • Tell people plainly they're talking to AI. The EU AI Act requires it for chatbots from August 2026.35
  • Don't design assistants to claim feelings to keep users engaged.

80,000 Hours: #5Read their profile: Great power conflict

AI and war between major powers

A race for AI can make a dangerous rivalry more likely to turn into war, and the war faster and deadlier.

Short answer

Not AI on its own. The risk is that AI makes an old danger worse: fear that a rival gets decisive AI first creates pressure to race, cut corners or strike. The profile's author, Stephen Clare, puts the chance of a new war between great powers in roughly the next 30 years at about one in three, as his own estimate.36

What's plausible

  • EstimateClare's best guess for an existential catastrophe caused directly by war in the next century is between 0.05% and 2%.36
  • EvidenceAI is already a source of tension between powers, for example through US export controls on advanced chips. The same profile notes real openings for cooperation on AI.36

What's uncertain

  • SpeculationAI in military command, including nuclear systems, could raise the risk of accidents, because AI can behave unexpectedly in situations it wasn't trained for.36
  • DisputedWhich way AI cuts. It could also help peace, for example by making agreements easier to verify.25 The scenario AI 2027 shows both paths, a race ending and a slowdown ending. Its authors call it a prediction, not a recommendation, and say it is one of many possible futures; one of them, Eli Lifland, has since put his median at about 2030.37

What you can do

You
  • Be wary of "race at any cost". Support talks and agreements between countries on AI, including ways to check them.
  • Consider it as a career option. 80,000 Hours says people who understand both geopolitics and AI are "sorely needed", for example on US–China relations or crisis management.36
Your organization
  • Map what geopolitics could cut off: chips, cloud regions and AI providers you depend on, and your fallback for each.
  • Work in defense or critical infrastructure? Keep a human decision on anything that escalates.

Also on the list: a reason for hope

AI that helps us decide better

80,000 Hours also lists using AI to improve how societies decide, through fact-checking, forecasting and tools that help groups negotiate and keep agreements.38 The argument, drawn partly from Forethought's "Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion", is that AI could compress a century of progress into a decade, so we'll need to make big decisions faster and better.39 The same list also includes 'S-risks' and space governance, which this page doesn't cover.

Part two

Where AI risk shows up today

Smaller stakes, known fixes. These are the places you and your organization are most likely to meet AI risk this year.

AI at work and in your business

The AI in daily use is rarely the AI that was approved.

Most organizations meet AI risk here first, and it is mostly ordinary: customer details pasted into a personal account, a wrong answer sent to a client, a tool nobody signed off. A one-page rule, an approved tool and a named person who checks fix most of it.

What we know

  • EvidenceIn Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 survey of 31,000 people in 31 countries, three in four knowledge workers used AI at work, and 78% of those brought their own tools.40
  • EvidenceA Canadian tribunal held Air Canada liable for its chatbot's wrong fare advice in 2024, and called the argument that the chatbot was a separate entity "remarkable".41

What to do

  • YouCheck before you paste. If you wouldn't email it to a stranger, don't put it into an unapproved AI tool.
  • Your organizationWrite the one-page rule. Approved tools, data that never goes in, when output must be checked, and who to ask.
  • Your organizationName an owner for every AI tool, who knows what it's used for and can switch it off.

AI governance and control

Who answers when an automated decision goes wrong.

There's no single AI rulebook and no single off-switch. Control is spread across existing law, the EU AI Act where it applies, your vendor contracts and your own internal controls. That last part is the one you can fix this quarter.

What we know

  • EvidenceThe EU AI Act's AI-literacy duty and bans on certain uses have applied since February 2025, and its transparency duties since August 2026.35 In July 2026 the EU moved the high-risk rules to 2 December 2027, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products.42
  • EvidenceThe UK has no single AI regulator; its AI Security Institute (renamed from AI Safety Institute in February 2025) tests advanced models rather than licensing them.43

What to do

  • YouAsk who owns it. If AI shapes a decision about you, ask how to challenge it and reach a person.
  • Your organizationList every AI system, including AI features inside software you already pay for.
  • Your organizationGive each one an owner and an off-switch, and test the off-switch once.

Cyber resilience

Faster attacks, same weak points: access, patches, backups.

AI mostly speeds up what attackers already do. The newer risk is AI agents connected to your email, files and systems, which hidden instructions in a web page or message can steer. Fewer permissions and backups you've restored cover most of both.

What we know

  • EstimateThe UK's National Cyber Security Centre judges AI will highly likely increase the volume and impact of cyber intrusions to 2027, mainly by improving existing techniques.44
  • EvidenceIn November 2025 Anthropic reported a state-backed espionage campaign that used its Claude model for 80–90% of the work against roughly thirty targets, a small number successfully.45 Prompt injection tops OWASP's 2025 list of risks for AI applications.46

What to do

  • YouProtect your email first with a passkey or two-step verification. It resets every other account.
  • Your organizationGive AI agents the least access that works. Read-only by default; a person approves payments, deletions and anything sent outside.
  • Your organizationPatch known holes fast. AI shortens the gap between a flaw being published and being exploited.

Jobs and careers

AI changes tasks before it changes job titles.

Probably not your job in one go, but likely some of your tasks, starting with the repetitive ones. The useful question is which tasks, and when. For a team it's the same question, asked task by task across everyone's week.

What we know

  • EstimateThe International Labour Organization estimates about one in four workers worldwide are in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, and expects most of those jobs to change rather than disappear.47
  • EvidenceStanford researchers found employment for 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations about 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers. They say they can't be sure AI caused it.48
  • EstimateResearchers at METR found the length of task AI agents can complete has doubled about every seven months since 2019, on software tasks; projecting it forward is contested.49

What to do

  • YouRun the one-task test. List your ten most common tasks. Mark which ones are repetitive and checkable. Try a good AI tool on one of them with a clock running.
  • Your organizationMap tasks, not job titles, starting with the roles that hire juniors.
  • Your organizationTrain before you buy more licenses. In the World Economic Forum's 2025 survey, 63% of employers named skill gaps as the main barrier to change.50

Verification and misinformation

Check where it came from, not how it looks.

You can't reliably spot AI-made images, video or text by eye, and a detector score isn't proof. Check the source, the original context and whether anyone independent confirms it. The same habit catches the confident wrong answers AI tools give you at work.

What we know

  • EvidenceOpenAI withdrew its own AI-text classifier in July 2023, citing "its low rate of accuracy".51
  • EvidenceContent Credentials (the C2PA standard) attach a signed record of how a file was made. They can be stripped, so a file without them proves nothing.52

What to do

  • YouFind the first version before you forward anything surprising, and look for two independent confirmations. Ten reposts of one source count as one.
  • YouIf it makes you angry or scared, wait an hour before you share it.
  • Your organizationNever decide on a detector score alone, in hiring, discipline or grading.

Scams and deepfakes

Voice clones, urgent transfers, and the call-back rule.

A familiar voice or face is no longer proof of who you're talking to. You don't need to work out whether it's fake. Pause, then confirm the person and the request through a route you already trust.

What we know

  • EvidenceIn early 2024 an employee of the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong transferred HK$200 million (about US$25 million) after a video call in which the "senior colleagues" were deepfakes.53
  • EvidenceThe FBI warned in December 2024 that criminals use AI-cloned voices of relatives "in an emergency", and advised families to agree a secret word.54

What to do

  • YouUse the call-back rule. Hang up, then call back on a number you already had, not one given in the message.
  • YouAgree a family code word this week, in person, including with older relatives.
  • Your organizationRequire two people and two channels for payments and any change of bank details, and say out loud that nobody gets in trouble for checking a senior person's request.

Privacy and identity

Lock the account that resets all the others.

AI makes stolen details, photos and voice clips easier to stitch into a convincing fake. Secure your email and phone accounts first, use passkeys where they're offered, and trim the details that help someone pass as you.

What we know

  • EvidenceIn April 2026 the UK's National Cyber Security Centre recommended passkeys over passwords wherever available, because they resist phishing.55
  • DisputedHow much public audio and video raises the risk for an ordinary person. The FBI advises limiting it,54 but changing how you verify matters more than hiding.

What to do

  • YouSecure the recovery chain: your main email, your phone number (ask your carrier for a port-out PIN) and your password manager.
  • YouCheck your AI chat settings: history, training use, and what's shared with connected apps.
  • Your organizationVerify identity by process, not by face or voice, especially for helpdesk password resets.

Preparedness

Plans that earn their keep even if nothing dramatic happens.

The sensible plan is ordinary: backups you've restored at least once, a way to reach family or staff when the usual apps fail, essentials for a few days, and income that doesn't hang on one thread. None of it depends on predicting what AI does next.

What we know

  • EvidenceIn July 2024 one faulty CrowdStrike update crashed about 8.5 million Windows devices, disrupting airlines, hospitals and banks.56 No AI was involved, which is the point: dependence is the risk.

What to do

  • YouWrite the household plan on paper: key numbers, where to meet, who relays messages if phones fail.
  • Your organizationList what stops if your main AI or cloud provider is down for a day, and who does that work by hand.

Worth doing whichever risk worries you

Six moves that help with all of these

Each is a no-regrets move: worth doing even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.

  • Get fluent. Use a good AI tool on real work each week, so you know first-hand where it helps and where it fails.
  • Check before you trust. Source, second channel, a person who answers for it.
  • Limit access. People and AI tools get the permissions the job needs, no more.
  • Keep a way back. Backups you've restored, a manual fallback, some financial runway.
  • Keep learning. Skills that move with you (judgment, people, knowing your field deeply) last longer than any one tool.
  • Be an informed citizen. The big risks in part one are decided by governments and companies. Know what yours are doing and say what you think.
