AI and jobs: what's actually going to happen?

Last checked November 2026 · About 9 minutes · For business owners, leaders, HR teams and anyone thinking about their own job

The short answer

Nobody knows how far or how fast this goes, and anyone who sounds certain is guessing. What we can see so far is AI taking over tasks inside jobs, and fewer young people being hired into the most exposed roles. There is no broad wave of AI layoffs in the data yet.

The first changes show up in hiring, not firing, and at the bottom of the ladder. Plan for that now, and keep watching, because the people who expect much faster change have serious arguments too.

Why people disagree

Serious people read the same evidence differently. In a 2025 Pew survey, 39% of AI experts expected AI to mean fewer US jobs over the next 20 years, and 19% expected more.13 Here are the four main positions, each at its strongest.

View 1

AI changes tasks, and most jobs adapt

AI takes over pieces of most office jobs and helps with others. Jobs get redesigned far more often than they disappear.

Who holds it
Researchers at the International Labour Organization, OpenAI, the University of Pennsylvania and Microsoft.
Strongest evidence
  • About 80% of US workers could have at least 10% of their tasks affected by AI tools like ChatGPT, but only about 19% could see half or more of their tasks affected.1
  • The ILO finds one in four workers worldwide in a job with some exposure, and 3.3% in the highest category. Its conclusion: "transformation of jobs is the most likely impact."2
  • In a sample of conversations from November 2025, 52% of conversations with Anthropic's Claude chatbot had people working with the AI, and 45% had them handing the task over.4
  • Microsoft researchers, after studying 200,000 conversations with Copilot, warned that reading high scores as job loss "would be a mistake": ATMs automated a core task of bank tellers, and teller jobs still grew.3
What it may miss
These are snapshots of today's tools. If AI keeps improving, the share of each job it can do rises with it.
View 2

Fast displacement, starting at the bottom

As AI gets more capable, companies stop hiring juniors, then cut upward through the hierarchy, faster than past technologies because AI can take on whole bundles of computer work.

Who holds it
Luke Drago and Rudolf Laine, whose essay series The Intelligence Curse (April 2025) calls it "pyramid replacement".5 Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, told Axios in May 2025 that AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs and push US unemployment to 10–20% within five years.6
Strongest evidence
  • The first thing it predicts has started to show. Stanford researchers find employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed jobs is 19% below trend, mostly through fewer hires rather than layoffs.10
  • Anthropic's economists find job-finding rates for 22 to 25 year olds into highly exposed jobs down about 14% since 2022, a result they call only barely statistically significant.11
What it may miss
The early data shows a pattern, not proof that AI caused it. And economy-wide data still shows no clear AI footprint.12
View 3

Slower than the demos suggest

Even when AI can do a task, using it at work costs money and time, and runs into quality problems, rules and liability. The change is real, and slow.

Who holds it
Researchers at MIT FutureTech, and MIT economist Daron Acemoglu.
Strongest evidence
  • When MIT researchers priced out computer-vision systems, only about 23% of the wages paid for vision tasks were attractive to automate at then-current costs.7
  • Acemoglu estimates AI will add no more than 0.66% to total productivity over ten years.8
  • In computer and math jobs, Anthropic found its AI used for 33% of tasks against 94% judged possible.11
What it may miss
Costs fall fast, and the MIT study covered image recognition only.
View 4

New work appears, as it always has

Technology removes some work and creates kinds we can't name yet. Most of today's jobs are in specialties that didn't exist in 1940.

Who holds it
Labor economists including MIT's David Autor, and the 19% of AI experts in Pew's survey who expected more jobs, not fewer.
Strongest evidence
  • Autor and colleagues find that "the majority of current employment is in new job specialties introduced after 1940."9
What it may miss
History doesn't say how long the gap lasts or who gets the new jobs. And if AI can learn the new work too, the old pattern may not hold.

Where they agree: routine computer work and entry-level roles change first. The argument is about speed, and how far up the ladder it goes.

What replacement could look like

One detailed forecast of how this unfolds is the "pyramid replacement" model in The Intelligence Curse.5 It is one view, not a consensus. Step through it and see what today's evidence says at each stage.

  1. Stage 1 of 6

    Hiring slows

    AI handles basic tasks, so firms hire fewer entry-level staff instead of laying people off.

    Evidence today: early signs

    Fewer 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed jobs (Stanford, data to June 2026), mainly through reduced hiring.10 Anthropic sees the same pattern, weakly.11

    Another reading: the Stanford authors call their findings "early, descriptive indicators," not proof of cause. Anthropic lists other explanations, such as young people returning to school.

  2. Stage 2 of 6

    Entry-level hiring stops

    Junior hiring shrinks fast as AI takes on more complex work.

    Evidence today: not yet clear

    We found no reliable data showing entry-level hiring has stopped in any broad field as of November 2026.

    Another reading: employers redesign junior roles around checking and customer work, and keep hiring.

  3. Stage 3 of 6

    Cuts reach management

    With no juniors coming up, layoffs move to the middle layers, often when a downturn gives companies the reason.

    Evidence today: not seen

    Yale's Budget Lab (14 October 2026): measures of AI usage "show no connection to changes in employment or unemployment."12

    Another reading: the brakes in View 3 hold this stage off for years, or for good.

  4. Stage 4 of 6

    AI does almost every role

    Most of the work below the top team is done by AI.

    Evidence today: speculation

    Needs AI far more capable and reliable than today's tools. Experts disagree on whether and when that arrives.

  5. Stage 5 of 6

    Only the top team remains

    A small leadership group directs AI systems that do the work.

    Evidence today: speculation

    Depends on the same leap in AI capability as stage 4.

  6. Stage 6 of 6

    No humans in daily operations

    In some firms, nobody manages the day-to-day.

    Evidence today: speculation

    The authors themselves say regulation, investor caution, unions and cost could slow all this, and that some work, such as long-range planning, taste and local knowledge, may last longer.5

Stage 1 of 6

Early signs some data points this way · Not yet clear no good data · Not seen data points the other way · Speculation depends on future AI

How to think about it

Four rules that hold whichever view turns out right.

  1. Think in tasks, not titles.

    Two people with the same title can have very different weeks. Ask which parts of the week are repetitive and easy to check.

  2. Watch hiring, not firing.

    The first signal is roles quietly not backfilled and junior intakes that shrink. Layoff headlines come later, if at all.

  3. Exposure isn't loss.

    Cost, quality, rules and who answers for mistakes all sit in between.

  4. Plan on the trend, not the snapshot.

    What AI can't do this year says little about next year. Recheck your plan every six months against what the tools can actually do.

What to do about it

Each list works whether change turns out fast or slow.

If you run a business

Run the one-task test.

Take your most repetitive task (quotes, replies, summaries). Do it your usual way with a clock running, then with a good AI tool. The minutes tell you more than any forecast.

Price the whole job, not the demo.

Count the checking, the fixing and the customers who want a person, not just the subscription.

Decide what the saved time is for.

More customers, better service or fewer hires. Choose on purpose and tell your staff before rumors do.

Hold the 3-year conversation.

One hour with your team: if AI could do our core service cheaply in three years, what do we sell then? Write down two trigger points: "if we see X, we do Y."

If you lead HR or L&D

Protect the entry-level pipeline.

Stop hiring juniors and in five years you'll have nobody ready to promote. Keep a junior intake, and build those roles around checking AI work, judgment and customer contact.

Track hiring by level.

Report entry-level roles not backfilled each quarter, next to headcount. It's the number that moves first.

Map tasks before you touch roles.

Use Which Parts of Your Team's Work Could AI Change First? to see where the change lands and what people should learn next.

Train people to check, not just to prompt.

Knowing when not to trust AI, and how to catch its mistakes, can be taught.

If you're thinking about your own job

Run the one-task test.

List your ten most common tasks. Mark the repetitive, checkable ones. Try a good AI tool on one with a clock running. That's your real exposure.

Check your job, task by task.

Check Your Job shows which of your tasks are within AI's reach, and when, if current trends hold.

Grow the part that needs you.

Ask for more of the sign-off, the client contact, the judgment.

Keep a cushion.

A few months of savings and an up-to-date CV, more so early in your career.

A no-regrets move: AI fluency, a financial cushion and skills that travel between roles are worth building even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.
