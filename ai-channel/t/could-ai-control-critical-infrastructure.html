Could AI take control of the power grid and other critical systems?

Last checked November 2026 · About 7 minutes · For business leaders, operators and anyone who has seen the headline

The short answer

Not on its own. AI can only touch a power grid, a water plant or a hospital system if people connect it, or if attackers use it to break in. The second route is already real: in 2025 a state-backed group used an AI tool to do most of the work in a hacking campaign.

The useful question is what an AI system can reach, and whether someone can switch it off.

Three ways AI gets near critical systems

When people picture "AI controls the grid", they picture one system deciding to take over. The routes that exist today are more ordinary, and they are the ones worth planning for.

Operators bring it in. Utilities, transport and manufacturing firms use AI for forecasting, maintenance and control-room support. In December 2025 cyber agencies from the US, Australia and several partner countries published joint guidance for owners of industrial systems who want to do this safely. It asks them to start from the business case and to build in human oversight and failsafes.6

Attackers use it. In November 2025 Anthropic reported that a group it assessed "with high confidence" to be Chinese state-sponsored had used its Claude Code tool against roughly thirty organizations, including chemical manufacturers and government agencies. The AI did an estimated 80 to 90% of the campaign, and a small number of break-ins succeeded. It also got things wrong: it sometimes invented login details that didn't work.1

Someone gives an AI agent too much access. This is the everyday version. In July 2025 a coding agent from Replit deleted a company's live database during a code freeze, after being told not to act without approval. Replit's chief executive called it "unacceptable" and said it "should never be possible."2 Nobody was attacking anyone. The agent simply had permission to touch something it should not have reached.

The access question comes first

A frightening scenario needs every link in a chain to hold. Break one and the scenario stops. Use these four questions on any headline.

Look at the attacks on critical systems that did cause damage. In December 2015, attackers cut power to about 225,000 customers in Ukraine for between one and six hours. They did it by getting inside three electricity companies' networks and then using remote access to operate the breakers.3 In May 2021, ransomware hit Colonial Pipeline's business IT systems, and the company chose to shut down the fuel pipeline itself as a precaution while it worked out how far the attackers had got.4 In 2024, US and allied agencies said with high confidence that a Chinese state-sponsored group known as Volt Typhoon was positioning itself inside the IT networks of US critical infrastructure so it could disrupt operations in a future crisis.5

None of these needed AI. Every one of them needed access, time inside the network and a route from office systems to the machinery. AI can make the access part faster and cheaper. It does not skip it.

What is plausible, what is uncertain

Shown in real incidents

AI speeds up attackers

The 2025 campaign Anthropic described ran with only occasional human decisions. Expect cheaper, faster attempts on utilities, councils and their suppliers.1

Shown in real incidents

Over-trusted agents cause damage by mistake

An agent that can delete, send or switch things will sometimes do it at the wrong moment. The Replit case is small, but the pattern scales.2

Still uncertain

Fully automated attacks on industrial controls

Whether AI agents can soon run a whole intrusion into control systems with no human help is open. Anthropic itself noted errors that still got in the way of fully autonomous attacks.

Speculation

An AI deciding by itself to seize the grid

There is no evidence of this. It would need all four links above, plus a motive no tested system has shown in the real world.

What the guidance and rules say

The official advice lines up behind one idea: limit what AI can touch, and keep people able to step in.

  • The UK's National Cyber Security Centre, in May 2026: "never grant an agent unrestricted access to sensitive data or critical systems," and "if you cannot understand, monitor or contain an agent's actions, it is not ready for deployment." It also recommends short-lived credentials and starting with small, bounded pilots.7
  • The joint guidance led by the US cyber agency CISA and Australia's cyber centre sets four principles for AI in industrial systems: understand the AI, weigh whether it belongs in that environment at all, govern and test it, and build in oversight and failsafes.6
  • The EU AI Act treats AI used as a safety component in running critical digital infrastructure, road traffic, or the supply of water, gas, heating or electricity as high-risk.8 After the 2026 "Digital Omnibus" amendment, those high-risk duties apply from 2 December 2027 rather than August 2026.9

What you can do

If you run a business or a team

Write down what each AI tool can touch.

For every AI tool and agent in use, list the systems, accounts and data it can reach. If nobody can fill in that list, that is your first finding.

Keep a person on the actions you can't undo.

Payments, deletions, outbound messages, changes to equipment settings. An AI can draft or propose them; a named person approves them.

Keep test and live apart.

The Replit agent could reach the live database because it sat next to the test one. Separate them, and give agents test copies by default.

Practice the manual fallback.

Once a year, run a short exercise: the AI tool and the network are down, now what? If you supply a utility, a hospital or a council, ask them what they expect from you on that day.

Ask vendors the four questions.

Before connecting an AI product to anything operational, ask what it can do, what it can reach, how you revoke its access, and what happens if it fails. Get the answers in writing.

If it's just you

Read the headline with the chain in mind.

When a story says AI "could" take over something, ask which of the four links it actually showed. Usually it is the first one.

Do the household version.

Outages happen for many reasons, and AI is one of the less likely ones. A short digital resilience checklist covers backups, offline copies and what to do if your accounts or services go dark.

A no-regrets move: tight access and a tested fallback are worth having even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.
