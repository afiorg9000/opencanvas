What is the AI singularity?

The idea explained without the mysticism: where it comes from, what is actually being measured, and why serious people disagree about whether it happens at all.

ArticleAbout 8 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

It's the idea that once AI can help build better AI, progress snowballs.

Each generation builds the next one faster, until change outruns our ability to forecast it or adapt to it. It's a hypothesis, not an event on a calendar. The first part of the loop is starting to be measured. Whether it runs away or hits the limits of chips, power and real-world testing is a genuine, open argument, and the honest answer to "when?" is a range of decades, not a date.

The idea in three steps

Today's AI tools are like very capable assistants: great at some tasks, hopeless at others. Companies are openly trying to build the next step, AI that can do most tasks a person can do at a computer. One of those tasks is AI research itself. That's the whole argument:

Insiders call this loop "recursive self-improvement" or an "intelligence explosion." The "singularity" is the point where, if the loop runs fast enough, the future stops being predictable from where we stand. It doesn't mean infinite intelligence. A feedback loop can speed things up a great deal and still level off.

The word itself is falling out of use. AISafety.info, a project founded by the AI safety educator Rob Miles, notes that "singularity" has been used inconsistently and that safety researchers now mostly talk about "takeoff speed": how fast AI goes from roughly human level to far beyond it.1

Where the idea comes from

It's older than ChatGPT by about sixty years. In 1965 the statistician I. J. Good wrote that "the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make." In 1993 the mathematician and novelist Vernor Vinge gave it a deadline:

"Within thirty years, we will have the technological means to create superhuman intelligence. Shortly after, the human era will be ended."Vernor Vinge, "The Coming Technological Singularity," 19932

Thirty years from 1993 was 2023. The date passed; the argument didn't. Ray Kurzweil, the best-known forecaster of the idea, has said since 2005 that it arrives around 2045, and in 2024 told an interviewer, "I'm sticking with that prediction."3 Dates come and go. That's a reason to watch the mechanism, not the calendar.

What is actually being measured

EvidenceResearchers at METR, a nonprofit that tests AI systems, measure how long a task AI agents can complete, judged by how long the same task takes a skilled person. Across six years, that length doubled roughly every seven months, and the trend may have sped up in 2024.4 By 2026 METR said its own task set could no longer reliably measure anything above about 16 hours: the measuring stick had started to run out.5 The catch: these are mostly self-contained software and research tasks with clear right answers, not the messy work most jobs involve.

EvidenceIs AI already speeding up the people who build AI? Harder to say than you'd think. In a 2025 trial, experienced software developers using AI tools took 19% longer on real tasks, while believing they'd been 20% faster.6 METR's 2026 follow-up concluded developers are "likely" sped up now, but couldn't measure by how much, partly because many developers refused to work without AI for the study.7 Even the people inside the loop misjudge its speed.

The argument: snowball or slow burn?

DisputedThe case for a snowball. William MacAskill and Fin Moorhouse, researchers at Forethought, argue that AI able to do research "could drive a century of technological progress over just a few years." Their main point isn't the technology. It's that many hard, lasting decisions (new weapons, concentrated power, how to govern all this) would arrive faster than our institutions are built to handle.8 A companion Forethought paper by Daniel Eth and Tom Davidson takes on the obvious objection, that chips can't be built fast enough. Their answer is that better software alone, on the same hardware, could keep the loop going for a while.9

DisputedThe case for a slow burn. Ege Erdil of Epoch AI argues that AI research is held back by computing power and data for experiments, not just by thinking time, so smarter AI alone can't make the loop run away. His central estimate is about 20 years until AI can do most remote work.10 Chips have to be built, data centers powered, and experiments run in real time. Epoch AI's own data show how much today's progress leans on that input: the computing power used to train leading language models has grown about five times a year since 2020 (as of February 2026).11

EstimateWhen? Treat every date as a bet.

  • A survey of 2,778 AI researchers, run by AI Impacts in late 2023, gave a 10% chance that machines outperform humans at every task by 2027 and a 50% chance by 2047. That 50% date had moved 13 years earlier in a single year.12
  • Forecasters on Metaculus have centered their date for general AI in the early 2030s (live forecast; it moves).13
  • Erdil's multi-decade view sits at the slower end; some people inside AI labs say 2030 or sooner.

The fork: a choice, not a date

In 2025 a team led by former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo published AI 2027, a detailed story of AI improving AI. Its unusual feature is that it has two endings from the same starting point. In one, a race between the US and China pushes everyone to cut corners. In the other, people slow down at a key moment and things go much better.14 The authors have since pushed some of their own estimates later and revised their model more than once, which is the right way to treat a forecast.

The point of the two endings is the useful part. Whether or not the loop arrives on their schedule, how it goes depends on choices people make before it does: testing, rules, and whether competitors agree to pause when it matters.

What you can do

You don't need to know whether the singularity is coming to make good decisions about it. Plan for a range.

For you

Pick a trigger, not a date.Decide what you'd have to see to change your plans ("AI does my hardest weekly task well") and check twice a year.
Run the one-task test.List your ten most common tasks. Try a good AI tool on one of them with a clock running. Repeat every few months; that's your personal trend line.
Build runway and skills that travel.Savings, judgment, working with people and learning fast are worth having in the fast scenario and the slow one.
Ask the questions you'd be embarrassed to ask.AISafety.info's Stampy chatbot answers questions about AI safety and links its sources; it's a prototype, so check them. Its Intro to AI safety explains why some researchers think the fast version would be dangerous, and How Can I Help lists ways to get involved.1

For your organization

Plan on the trend, not the snapshot.When you decide what not to automate, assume tools will handle longer tasks next year than this year, and check that assumption on a schedule.
Write the if-then rule."If a competitor halves its delivery time with AI, we trial the same tools within a month." One page, two triggers, reviewed twice a year.
Practice deciding faster.If change speeds up, slow approval cycles become the bottleneck. A tabletop exercise and pre-agreed thresholds help either way.

Each of these is a no-regrets move: worth doing even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.
