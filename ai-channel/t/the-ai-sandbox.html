How do we use AI tools without exposing our data?

Your people need outside AI tools. Every prompt is a small decision about where your data goes. Here are the five ways to control that, what each one really protects, and how to pick.

About 12 minutesLast checked November 2026Vendor-neutral

The short answer

Don't choose between banning AI and hoping for the best. Sort your data into what can go out, what can go out only under contract, and what must stay in. Then give people an approved route for each.

For most businesses that means a business-tier AI agreement that rules out training on your data, plus a short list of things that never go in. Private or on-premises models are for the small slice of data that can't leave at all. Only that last option keeps data on your own systems; the others control what leaves and on what terms.

Why this can't wait

If you haven't given people an approved AI tool, many are already using their own. In Microsoft and LinkedIn's 2024 survey of knowledge workers, 78% of people who use AI at work said they bring their own tools.1 Their own tools means their own accounts, under consumer terms you never agreed to.

That has a cost. In IBM's 2025 breach study, one in five organizations reported a breach linked to "shadow AI" (tools staff use without approval), and organizations with a lot of it paid about $670,000 more per breach on average.2 The best-known early case is Samsung, which restricted generative AI on company devices in 2023 after engineers pasted internal source code into ChatGPT.3

A ban on its own rarely fixes this. People route around rules that make their job harder. The fix is a better route than their own account.

What a "sandbox" actually is

There's no single product called an AI sandbox. It's a controlled zone you build out of three things:

  • Approved tools and accounts, on terms you have read.
  • Data rules that say which kinds of information can go into which tool.
  • A record of what was used, so you can check and learn.

The five approaches below are the building blocks. Most organizations end up combining two or three of them.

The five approaches

1 · Contract

Business agreements with no-training terms

Move people from personal accounts to a business or API plan whose terms say your prompts and files aren't used to train the provider's models. The major providers say this for their business products: OpenAI for its API, Microsoft for Copilot under its enterprise data protection terms, and Google for Workspace unless you give permission.456

Protects against
Your data turning up in someone else's answers via training; unclear consumer terms.
Doesn't protect against
Your data still leaves your network. "No training" isn't "no storage": OpenAI, for example, keeps API logs for up to 30 days for abuse monitoring unless you're approved for zero retention.4
Effort and cost
Low. Licences plus an afternoon reading the data terms.
Good for
Everyday drafting, summarizing and analysis of internal material.

Read for: retention period, where data is processed, sub-processors, who at the vendor can see it, and what changes when someone turns on web search or a third-party plugin.

2 · Habit

Data minimization and redaction

Send only what the task needs. A tool can rewrite a complaint letter without the customer's name, account number or address. Redaction can be manual (a rule people follow) or automated (a tool that strips names, numbers and IDs before the prompt is sent). The UK data protection regulator expects this kind of minimization when AI touches personal data.11

Protects against
Oversharing. It works with any tool and costs almost nothing.
Doesn't protect against
Context. Automated redaction catches patterns (card numbers, emails) and misses meaning ("our biggest client in Lyon is about to leave"). People also forget.
Effort and cost
Low for the rule; moderate for automated tools.
Good for
Every organization, as a baseline on top of whatever else you choose.
3 · Transformation

Pseudonymization and anonymization

Pseudonymization swaps identifiers for tokens ("Customer 4471") and keeps the key on your side, so you can put names back into the answer afterwards. Anonymization removes the link for good. Both sound like they take the data out of privacy law. Usually they don't.

The UK and EU regulators say pseudonymized data is still personal data for whoever holds or can use the key.78 In 2025 the EU's top court added that whether it's personal data for a recipient depends on whether that recipient can realistically re-identify people.9 That helps the vendor's position, not yours: you hold the key, so for you it stays personal data. True anonymization is hard to achieve with rich text, where details like job titles and dates can identify someone.

Protects against
Direct exposure of names and IDs; lowers the impact if a vendor is breached.
Doesn't protect against
Re-identification from context; your obligations under privacy law.
Effort and cost
Moderate. Needs tooling, a secure key store and testing.
Good for
Bulk analysis of customer feedback, case notes or tickets.
4 · Location

Private or on-premises models

Run an open-weight model (one whose files anyone can download) on your own servers or in your own cloud account. This is the only approach where prompts don't go to a model provider at all. As the UK AI Security Institute puts it, open-weight models "can be hosted privately, with no data returning to the model provider."10

Protects against
Data leaving your environment; changes to a vendor's terms.
Doesn't protect against
Your own security gaps. You now own patching, access control, logging and testing. Open models also trail the best closed ones: on the Institute's cyber tests, the leading open models matched closed models released 4 to 7 months earlier (July 2026).10
Effort and cost
High. Hardware or cloud compute, engineers, and ongoing upkeep.
Good for
The data that truly can't leave: regulated records, source code, legal privilege, trade secrets.

Going this route? Read In-house LLM governance first.

5 · Control point

An AI gateway with data loss prevention

Put one route between your people and every AI tool. A gateway (or the data loss prevention features in your existing security tools) can block unapproved services, warn or strip sensitive patterns before a prompt leaves, and log use. It turns your data rules into something the system enforces.

Protects against
Accidental pastes of obvious secrets; use of unapproved tools on managed devices.
Doesn't protect against
Personal phones and home laptops; sensitive meaning that doesn't match a pattern. The logs are now sensitive data too.
Effort and cost
Moderate to high. Worth it once many people use AI daily.
Good for
Mid-size and larger organizations, regulated sectors, and anyone who has to show an auditor what happened.

What a sandbox doesn't fix

Controlling what goes in is half the job. Three risks sit outside it.

  • What comes out. A tool that's private can still be wrong. Someone still has to check the answer before it reaches a customer. See When AI gets it wrong.
  • What the AI reads. Once an assistant can read email, web pages or shared drives, hidden instructions inside that content can steer it, and leak what it can see. The UK National Cyber Security Centre warns this "prompt injection" may never be fully solved, so limit what such a tool can reach and do.12 It's top of the OWASP list of risks for AI applications, with sensitive information disclosure second.13
  • What gets connected. Each plugin, connector or agent that links an AI tool to your systems widens what it can see. Approve connections the way you approve tools.

What you can do

For your organization

  1. Write the three-column list. Can go into an approved tool · Can go in only with names stripped · Never goes into an outside tool. One page. Use examples from your own work, not categories.
  2. Give people a better route than their own account. Pick one approved business tool, read its data terms, and switch it on before you announce any rule.
  3. Read the data terms, not the brochure. Find the retention period, the processing location and the sub-processor list. Write them down where your team can see them.
  4. Keep the crown jewels out until you're ready. If something truly can't leave, it stays out of outside tools until you have a private setup or a contract your lawyers have signed off.
  5. Review every quarter. Terms, tools and features change. Put a 30-minute check in the calendar.

For you, at your desk

  1. Run the name test. Before you paste, ask: would I be comfortable if this showed up with my name on it in a vendor's logs? If not, strip it or don't send it.
  2. Use the work account. Never move work data into a personal AI account, even "just this once."
