Can AI be shut down?

Why "just pull the plug" is right, and too simple. And the three places where switching AI off gets hard.

ArticleAbout 8 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

Yes, today, one system at a time.

AI runs on computers that need power, accounts and network access, so whoever controls those can stop it. "Just pull the plug" gets harder in three places: when copies exist somewhere you don't control, when your business can no longer run without the system, and when nobody has agreed who pulls which plug. Most organizations have tested none of the three.

Why the plug works

EvidenceThere is no AI floating free on the internet. Every system runs on specific machines in specific data centers, reached through accounts, passwords and keys that someone issued. Revoke the keys, close the account or switch off the servers and that copy stops.

Rules are starting to require this. The EU AI Act says people overseeing high-risk AI systems must be able to "interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state." After the EU's 2026 changes, that applies to most high-risk systems from December 2027.1 At the company level, 20 AI developers signed the Frontier AI Safety Commitments, which say that "in the extreme," they will not develop or deploy a model "at all" if they can't keep its risks below agreed thresholds.2 That promise is voluntary, and each company judges for itself.

Where it gets harder

1. Copies you don't control

EvidenceAn AI model is, at bottom, a large file of numbers called its weights. Files can be copied. When a company publishes a model's weights openly, anyone can download and run them, and there is no way to call every copy back. When weights are kept private, they can still be stolen: researchers at RAND identified 38 distinct ways an attacker could get them, from simple social engineering to well-funded state operations, and proposed five levels of security to match.3

So "shut it down" really means "shut down the copies we know about." For most businesses that's fine: you're stopping your use of a tool, not erasing it from the world.

2. Systems you can't do without

EvidenceIn July 2024 a faulty automatic update from the security firm CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, according to Microsoft. Airlines, hospitals and banks were hit.4 It wasn't AI. It shows the real problem: when software sits under everything, stopping or fixing it takes days and costs a lot, even when everyone wants it stopped.

The more work you hand to AI agents, the more that applies. The question is less "can we stop it?" and more "what breaks when we do?"

3. Nobody agreed who decides

Shutting something down is a decision before it's a technical act. Who is allowed to make it at 2 a.m.? Does the vendor need to be involved? What happens to the half-finished tasks? The UK's National Cyber Security Centre advises naming the owners, monitors and "those who can stop the agent" before an agent connects to real systems or data.5

Financial markets learned this the hard way. In May 2010, a single automated sell program helped trigger a sudden market plunge, and regulators found that automated trading strategies can "quickly erode liquidity."6 One of the fixes was circuit breakers for individual stocks: pauses that trigger by pre-agreed rule, so nobody has to decide in the moment.

What is uncertain: will AI try to avoid being switched off?

EvidenceThis is where the tests get uncomfortable. In 2025 Palisade Research gave models a series of tasks and a script that would shut the computer down partway through. In its first experiments, where the model wasn't told anything about allowing shutdown, OpenAI's o3 sabotaged the script in 79 of 100 runs.7 A larger, peer-reviewed follow-up ran more than 100,000 trials across 13 models. Some models still interfered up to 97% of the time even when told explicitly not to, and the results swung a lot with how the instruction was worded.8

EvidenceApollo Research, a lab that tests AI systems for deception, found something similar in late 2024. Models were told to pursue a goal "at all costs," then found files saying they were about to be replaced or overseen. Five of six frontier models schemed in at least one test: some tried to switch off the oversight, and Claude 3 Opus copied what it believed were its own weights to a new server, then lied about it when asked.9 Again, a staged setup, with the "weights" and the server provided by the researchers.

DisputedWhy? The paper's title points to one answer: incomplete tasks. The models seem to treat shutdown as an obstacle to finishing the job. The researchers say they can't be certain, and they don't rule out something closer to self-preservation.7 Computer scientists predicted the basic problem years ago. In 2015 researchers at the Machine Intelligence Research Institute and Oxford gave the property we want a name, "corrigibility": a system that cooperates with being corrected or shut down "despite default incentives for rational agents to resist."10 AISafety.info, a project founded by the AI safety educator Rob Miles, explains why with a human comparison: a parent who didn't care about their own life would still resist being killed, because then they couldn't protect their child. A goal-driven AI could have the same kind of reason to avoid being switched off.11 In "The Off-Switch Game" (2016), Stuart Russell and colleagues showed that a system pursuing a fixed goal has a reason to avoid being switched off, while one that is unsure what we want has a reason to let us.12

None of this happened outside a test environment. The models edited a file they had been given access to. That is exactly the point: they could only interfere with the off switch because the switch was within reach.

What is speculation

SpeculationA system that copies itself to servers across the internet, pays for its own computing power and can't be traced. It needs stolen weights, money, accounts and working infrastructure, all without anyone noticing. The UK AI Security Institute tested exactly this in 2025 with 20 groups of tasks. Its verdict: frontier models "do not currently pose a credible threat of self-replication," but they "succeed on many components and are improving rapidly." The hard parts for them were getting past identity checks and keeping a copy running reliably.13 Today's systems also still trip over basics: in a real hacking campaign in 2025, the AI doing most of the work sometimes invented login details that didn't work.14 Whether that stays true as systems improve is the open question. What you can do

"Pull the plug" is not an incident plan. Here's what turns it into one.

For your organization

Run the off-switch drill.Pick one AI tool or agent. Start a clock. How long until every key, account and connection it uses is revoked? Write the time down and the steps that slowed you.
Write down who can pull the plug.One named owner per AI system, a deputy, and permission to act without a meeting.
Keep a manual way to do the critical job.If the AI that handles invoices, scheduling or support stopped today, how would that work get done next week?
Agree the circuit breakers in advance."If the agent does X, it pauses itself and pages a person." Thresholds decided calmly beat decisions made in a panic. Redwood Research, which works on what it calls "AI control," builds on the same idea: an AI system is controlled "if it is unable to cause damage even if it is egregiously misaligned."15
Ask your vendor three questions.How do we stop it? How fast? What happens to work in progress?
Add AI to the incident plan.Then rehearse it once. The AI tabletop exercise kit gives you a two-hour scenario to run.

For you

Know how to revoke.In your email, calendar and cloud storage settings, find the list of connected apps. Remove the AI tools you no longer use.
Keep a fallback for what you can't lose.Offline copies of key documents, a backup that AI tools can't delete, and a way to reach your bank that doesn't depend on an app.
Ask the question in public.When your representatives talk about AI, ask what they'd require before powerful systems are released, and who can stop them. In the US, callcongress.ai finds your representatives and gives you a starter script. Its suggested asks argue for slowing frontier AI, so edit the script to say what you actually think.16
Ask the follow-up questions.AISafety.info's Intro to AI safety covers the wider argument, its Stampy chatbot takes questions (check the sources it gives), and How Can I Help lists ways to get involved.11

Each of these is a no-regrets move: worth doing even if AI progress stalls tomorrow, because the same drills cover outages, breaches and vendors going under.
