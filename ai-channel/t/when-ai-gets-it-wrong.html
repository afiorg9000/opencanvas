What should you do when AI gets it wrong at work?

Catching it, checking it, fixing it, and who's accountable. Plus a two-minute checking routine your team will actually use.

About 10 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

Treat it like any other mistake with your name on it: stop it spreading, fix it, tell the people it reached, and change the step that let it through.

The AI isn't accountable. The person or business that used its output is, and courts and tribunals have said so plainly. The cheapest fix comes before anything goes out: a short checking routine matched to how much the output matters.

1 · Accountability

Whose mistake is it?

"The AI wrote it" has been tried as a defense. It hasn't worked.

When Air Canada's website chatbot told a grieving customer he could claim a bereavement discount after flying, which wasn't the airline's policy, the airline argued in front of a Canadian tribunal that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions." The tribunal called that "a remarkable submission" and held the airline responsible for everything on its website, chatbot included. It ordered the airline to pay the difference in fare plus interest and fees, about CA$812 in all.1 The money was small. The principle wasn't: what your bot says, your business said.

Courts have taken the same line with professionals. In New York in 2023, two lawyers and their firm were fined US$5,000 for filing a brief that cited cases ChatGPT had invented.2 In June 2025, England's High Court dealt with a case where 18 of 45 cited authorities didn't exist; the client had generated them with AI tools, and the court held that relying on the client's research didn't absolve the solicitor.3 One researcher's database of court decisions involving AI-made-up content had passed 2,262 cases by November 2026.4 And in October 2025, Deloitte agreed to partially refund the Australian government for a A$440,000 report that contained references to studies that didn't exist and a made-up quote from a court judgment.5

The pattern is consistent: the tool doesn't carry the responsibility; the person who sent the work does, and so does the organization behind them.

2 · Know what you're looking for

The six ways AI answers go wrong

"Hallucination" gets the headlines, but it's one of several. Knowing which kind you're dealing with tells you how to check. (For the why, read why AI sounds so confident when it's wrong.)

Made up
A citation, quote, statistic, policy or person that doesn't exist, delivered in the same calm tone as everything else.
Out of date
True once. Prices, rules, product features, who holds which job.
Misread
It had the right document and still got it wrong: a dropped "not," a figure from the wrong row, a summary that flips the conclusion.
Miscounted
Totals, percentages, dates and unit conversions. Fluent text around a wrong number.
Wrong question
A good answer to a question you didn't quite ask, or one built on a false premise you slipped into the prompt.
Telling you what you want
AI assistants tend to agree with the person asking. Research on this "sycophancy" found it's a general behavior of today's assistants, partly because people rate agreeable answers higher.6 Push back and it may change a right answer to a wrong one.
3 · Catching it

Catching it: the red flags

The hard part is that wrong answers don't look wrong. A survey of 319 knowledge workers found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported putting in.7 So build the habit around signals, not gut feeling. Slow down when you see:

  • Very specific details you didn't supply. An exact figure, a page number, a named study, a direct quote.
  • A name, case or source you've never heard of, in a field you know reasonably well.
  • No source, or a source that doesn't say it. Open the link. Find the sentence.
  • A confident answer to something nobody could know, such as an internal figure, a private person's details or next year's price.
  • It agreed with you a little too easily, especially after you pushed back.
  • It changes when you ask again. Ask the same thing in a fresh chat. If the details move, none of them are safe.
4 · Checking it

Checking it: match the check to the stakes

Checking everything to the same standard is how checking stops happening. Ask one question first: who sees this, and what happens if it's wrong?

Low stakes

Only you, easy to undo

Brainstorms, rough notes, rewording your own text.

Check: read it properly. Does it make sense?

Medium stakes

Colleagues or a customer

Client emails, internal reports, summaries people will act on.

Check: every fact, figure, name and quote against a source you opened yourself.

High stakes

Money, law, health, the public

Payments, contracts, legal or medical content, regulators, anything that can't be undone.

Check: medium checks plus a second person. AI can draft; a person decides.

Four ways to check that actually work

  • Go to the source. Open the document, law, price list or record and find the exact line. A link the AI gives you is a lead, not proof.
  • Redo the numbers. Recalculate totals and percentages in a spreadsheet or calculator. It takes a minute.
  • Ask fresh, or ask another tool. Disagreement is a useful warning. Agreement isn't proof; tools often share the same blind spots.
  • Ask someone who knows. Two minutes with the colleague who owns the subject beats twenty minutes of searching.
5 · Correcting it

It already went out: the first hour

Fast and plain beats perfect and late.

  1. Stop it spreading. Unsend if you can, take the page down, pause the automation or chatbot answer, and tell anyone about to act on it.
  2. Find everywhere it went. Who received it? Was it copied into a template, a knowledge base, a proposal or a help-center article? AI errors travel well.
  3. Fix it and say so plainly. "The figure in yesterday's email was wrong. The correct figure is X. Sorry for the confusion." Don't blame the tool; it reads as dodging.
  4. Put it right for anyone affected. If a customer relied on what your chatbot or email told them, deal with it as you would any promise your business made. That's the Air Canada lesson.
  5. Write down what happened. What was wrong, how it was found, which check would have caught it. Three lines is enough.
  6. Change the step, not just the sentence. Add the check that was missing, for that whole kind of task. Fixing one email fixes one email.

When it's bigger than an email. If the error involved personal data, a regulated decision, a contract or a large payment, bring in whoever handles legal, data protection or finance for you, today. Obligations vary by country and sector, and some have deadlines.

6 · Set it up

Who's accountable, and how to set it up

Answering "who's accountable when AI gets something wrong?" after the fact is uncomfortable. Answer it now, while nothing's wrong.

The sender owns the output
Whoever sends, publishes or acts on AI work is responsible for it, exactly as if they'd written it. Say this out loud, once, to everyone.
Every AI use has an owner
The customer chatbot, the meeting summaries, the proposal drafts: each has one named person who decides how it's checked and gets told when it goes wrong.
Managers own the routine
If people are told to "use AI more" without being shown how to check it, the errors belong partly to whoever set the target.
"Human in the loop" means a real check
People rubber-stamp output they see all day. The EU AI Act names this "automation bias" and requires people overseeing high-risk systems to be made aware of it.8 The same is true of any team: a reviewer with no time or authority to say no isn't a check.
Vendors won't carry it for you
Read what the contract says about accuracy before you rely on it. Expect it to say the output is yours to check.
7 · For teams

The two-minute check

A checking routine only works if people use it on a busy Tuesday. This one fits on a card. It matters more than it sounds: in a 2025 survey of US desk workers, 40% said they'd received AI-generated work in the past month that looked finished but wasn't, leaving someone else to fix it.9

Team card

Before AI work leaves your hands: the two-minute check

1. Who sees this, and what if it's wrong?
Only me → read it properly. Colleague or customer → steps 2–5. Money, law, health, public → steps 2–6.
2. Circle the checkable bits
Every fact, figure, name, date, quote and link.
3. Check each one against a source you opened
Find the actual line. Redo the sums.
4. Read it as the person receiving it
Does it answer what they asked? Would you sign it?
5. Say AI helped, if it did
"First draft with AI; figures checked by me."
6. High stakes: second pair of eyes
AI can draft. A person decides.
If one gets through
Stop it, fix it, tell people, write three lines, change the step. Tell
Prints the card only. Pin it up, or paste it into your team's AI rules.

Keep it alive: the wrong answer of the month

Once a month, someone shares one real wrong AI answer from your own work: what it said, how it was caught, what the check was. Nothing teaches checking faster than a confident mistake in your own field, and it makes catching errors something people get credit for, not something they hide.

8 · Actions

What you can do

For you
  • Circle before you send. On your next AI-assisted piece, circle every fact, figure, name and quote and check each one. Time it. Most people find it's under five minutes.
  • Run the fresh-chat test. When an answer matters, ask again in a new conversation. If the details move, check all of them.
  • Hold your ground. When you push back and the AI instantly agrees, treat that as a reason to check, not as confirmation.
For your team
  • Draw the "human decides" line. List the actions that can't be undone: money moving, records deleted, offers made, anything sent to a regulator. AI can draft; a person decides.
  • Name an owner for each AI use. One person per chatbot, workflow or recurring task.
  • Pin up the two-minute check. Then start the wrong answer of the month at your next team meeting.
  • See how exposed you are. The Business AI Exposure Check has a whole section on who checks the work.
