Would your city cope with a serious AI incident? Rehearse it in two hours.Would your leadership team cope with a serious AI incident? Rehearse it in two hours.

Two hours with the people who'd be in the room on a bad day. Eight AI incidents arrive one at a time, you decide with half the facts, and you leave with a short list of gaps to fix. No technical knowledge needed. Fire drills work because people practice before the fire.

Part 1 · The exercise

What this exercise is, and who should be in the room

You'll leave with

  • A clear view of who decides what when AI goes wrong
  • The information you wished you'd had (your gap list)
  • Three to five owned actions with dates
  • A calm, specific way to talk about AI risk as a team

Who to invite (6–12 people)

  • Mayor, chief executive or deputy (decision-maker)
  • City manager / chief operating officer
  • Communications lead and a press officer
  • IT / digital lead and information-security lead
  • Data-protection officer and legal counsel
  • Heads of a frontline service (e.g. social services, benefits)
  • HR, finance and emergency-management leads
  • Chief executive (decision-maker)
  • Chief operating officer and chief financial officer
  • Technology lead and information-security lead
  • Legal / compliance and data-protection lead
  • Communications / marketing lead
  • HR lead and customer-service lead
  • Optionally: a board member as observer

What you need

  • A room for 2.5 hours (2 hours plus set-up), a timer, a whiteboard or flipchart
  • One facilitator (ideally not the most senior person in the room), one note-taker, optionally one observer
  • Printed scenario cards (Part 4), one debrief worksheet per participant (Part 5)
  • Whatever you already have: a list of AI tools in use, AI or IT policies, your incident or crisis plan, comms protocols. If something's missing, that's a finding, not a failure.

Why rehearse this now? None of these incidents needs science-fiction AI. AI already sits inside everyday software, a convincing fake video of a public figure costs almost nothing, and in the EU the AI Act's AI-literacy duty (Article 4) has applied since February 2025 and its transparency duties (Article 50) since August 2026.

Part 3 · Facilitator guide

How to run it well

Two to three weeks before

  • Agree the purpose with the most senior sponsor: "find our gaps", not "test people".
  • Invite the group and confirm one decision-maker will attend for the full two hours.
  • Ask for the materials listed in Part 1. If nobody can produce a list of AI tools in use, note that as finding number one.
  • Make the injects local: swap in your real services, systems and channels. Use roles, never people's names.
  • Brief the note-taker: write down decisions, who made them, what nobody knew, and where people disagreed. Not who "did badly."

Ground rules to read out at the start

  • No-fault. We're testing the system, not the people. Gaps are the point.
  • Stay in your real role. Decide what you would actually decide, with what you'd actually know.
  • No fighting the scenario. "That would never happen" goes in the parking lot; play it anyway.
  • Decide under uncertainty. You won't get perfect information. Neither would you on the day.
  • Phones away except for the comms lead drafting statements.

Delivering each inject

  1. Read the situation aloud, then hand out the card (participant version, without notes).
  2. Start the timer. Ask the decision prompts one by one; push for a decision and an owner.
  3. If the team settles too quickly, use the twist. If they're stuck, offer one extra fact.
  4. At time, sum up in one sentence what was decided and what's unresolved. Move on.

What good looks like, in any inject

  • Someone clearly owns each decision, and the group knows who that is within a minute.
  • People ask "how do we know this is real?" before acting.
  • There's a manual fallback for anything AI runs, and someone knows how to switch to it.
  • Communications are fast, honest about what isn't known, and come from one official source.
  • Staff who used AI badly are treated as a process failure first, not a disciplinary case first.

Within a week after

  • Send a one-page write-up: what we decided, gaps found, actions with owners and dates.
  • Book a 30-minute check-in at 60 days on the actions.
  • Run it again in 6–12 months with new injects. What AI can do is changing fast, so the scenarios should too.

Some injects (job cuts, staff anxiety) can touch real worries in the room. Acknowledge them, keep the tone practical, and point people to employee-assistance or other professional support if the conversation becomes personal.

Where to begin

Five things to do whatever the exercise shows

Each one is a no-regrets move: worth doing even if AI progress stalls tomorrow.

  1. Build AI fluency at the top. Leaders who use AI themselves ask better questions about it.
  2. Know what you run. Keep a simple list of AI systems and tools in use, each with a named owner.
  3. Make checking normal. A call-back rule, two approvals for payments, and one official channel for statements.
  4. Keep a fallback. For every service that depends on AI, know how to run it without.
  5. Tell your people early. Training and straight talk beat surprise announcements.
