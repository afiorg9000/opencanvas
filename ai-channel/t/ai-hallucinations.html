Explainer · AI hallucinations

Why does AI sound so confident when it's wrong?

What hallucinations are, why they happen, when they matter most, and how to get fewer of them.

About 9 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

Because sounding right and being right are different skills, and AI tools are much better at the first. They write the most plausible next words, and the tests used to grade them have long rewarded a confident guess over "I don't know." So a made-up answer comes out in the same calm voice as a correct one.

It's getting better in places, but it won't disappear. The useful question is when it matters, and how to catch it.

1 · The idea

What a hallucination is

A hallucination is when an AI tool states something false as if it were fact: a court case that never happened, a statistic nobody measured, a quote nobody said, a feature your product doesn't have. The US standards body NIST prefers the word "confabulation" and defines it as "confidently stated but erroneous or false content," known colloquially as hallucinations or fabrications.1

Two things make it different from an ordinary mistake. First, it often looks more convincing than a true answer, because it's specific and tidy. Second, the tool isn't lying in the human sense; it has no idea it's wrong. (Whether AI can deceive on purpose is a separate question: see Can AI lie?)

2 · The mechanism

Why it happens: four reasons

  1. It predicts; it doesn't look things up. Tools like ChatGPT learn the patterns of enormous amounts of text and then produce the words most likely to come next. NIST calls confabulation "a natural result" of that design.1 Most of the time the likely answer is also the true one. When the tool hasn't seen enough about something, the likely-sounding answer can be pure invention.
  2. The tests reward guessing. Researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech compare it to a multiple-choice exam: a student who guesses scores better than one who leaves a blank. Most AI benchmarks grade the same way, so "I don't know" scores zero while a confident guess sometimes scores a point. Training follows the scores. Asked for one of the authors' birthdays, one model gave three different wrong dates in three tries.2
  3. The "I know this" switch misfires. Researchers at Anthropic looked inside one of their models and found that declining to answer is its default; a "known entity" signal switches that off when it recognizes a subject. Sometimes it recognizes a name without actually knowing anything about the person, the switch flips anyway, and the model makes something up.3
  4. It's trained to please. AI assistants are tuned partly on which answers people prefer, and people tend to prefer answers that agree with them. Research on this "sycophancy" found it's a general behavior of today's assistants.4 Ask a leading question and you often get a confident yes.

So why the confident tone?

Because the tone isn't connected to the accuracy. The tool writes a fluent, assured sentence the same way whether the facts inside it are solid or invented. People read confidence as a sign of knowledge. With AI, it's mostly a sign of style.

3 · Evidence

Is it getting better?

In places, yes. Newer isn't automatically more accurate, and the rate depends heavily on the task.

Fairly clear

Newer models aren't always better

On OpenAI's own test of questions about real people, its April 2025 reasoning models hallucinated more than the model before them:5

o1 (older)
16% of answers
o3
33%
o4-mini (smaller)
48%

OpenAI said o3 "tends to make more claims overall," right and wrong, and that more research was needed to understand why.

Fairly clear

Specialist tools help, but don't solve it

Stanford researchers found general chatbots hallucinated on 58% to 82% of legal questions. Specialist legal research tools, built to answer from real case law, did better but still got it wrong more than 17% of the time, and one more than 34%.6

In news, a study by 22 public broadcasters in 18 countries found 45% of AI assistants' answers about the news had at least one significant problem; about a fifth had major accuracy issues, including invented or outdated details.7

Still uncertain

Can it be fixed?

The OpenAI and Georgia Tech researchers argue rates can come down if tests stop punishing "I don't know."2 That would mean tools that answer less often but are wrong less often. Nobody serious claims it can reach zero for a system that generates text by prediction.

Still uncertain

What's your error rate?

Published figures come from specific tests, models and dates; they go out of date fast and aren't your error rate. The only number that tells you about your work is one you measure on your own tasks (see "Run the twenty-question test" below).

4 · Where to be careful

When it matters most

Hallucinations aren't spread evenly. They cluster where the tool has little to go on and where you're least able to check.

Riskier
  • Citations, quotes and references. The classic. They look real and often aren't.
  • Niche facts. Smaller companies, local rules, less-famous people, your own organization.
  • Numbers. Statistics, calculations, dates, prices.
  • Recent events. Anything after the tool's training, unless it's searching the web, and even then.
  • Long documents. Summaries can drop a "not" or invent a conclusion.
  • Leading questions. "Why did X happen?" when X didn't.
Safer
  • Brainstorming, where a wrong idea costs nothing.
  • Rewriting your own text, where you know what it should say.
  • Formatting and structure: tables, outlines, tone changes.
  • Drafts in your own field, where you'll spot what's off.
  • Working only from a document you supplied, and then checking against it.

The rule of thumb: the less you could check it yourself, the more it needs checking. For what to do when one gets through at work, see What should you do when AI gets it wrong at work?

5 · Practical

How to get fewer of them

You can't switch hallucinations off. You can make them rarer and easier to spot.

When you ask

  • Give it the source. Paste or attach the document and say "answer only from this; if it isn't there, say so."
  • Ask where it found it. Request the exact quote and where it appears, so you can find the line yourself.
  • Make "I don't know" allowed. Say it out loud in the prompt: "If you're not sure, say you're not sure."
  • Ask neutral questions. "What happened to X?" rather than "Why did X fail?"
  • Break big jobs up. Smaller, specific questions are easier to answer and to check.

When you check

  • Check the specifics. Names, numbers, dates, quotes and links: open the source, find the sentence.
  • Ask again in a fresh chat. Real facts tend to stay put. Invented ones tend to move.
  • Don't treat a link as proof. Tools that search the web still misquote what they find.

If you're choosing tools or setting them up for others

  • Prefer tools that show their sources, and spot-check that the sources say what's claimed.
  • Connect it to your own documents for questions about your own policies and products, then test it on questions where you know the answer.
  • Keep a person on anything high-stakes. Money, legal, health, public statements. AI can draft; a person decides.
  • Measure it on your work. Vendor benchmarks don't tell you how a tool does on your questions.
6 · Actions

What you can do

For you
  • Catch one on purpose. Ask your usual AI tool about something you know very well: your town, your field, your own company. Note what it gets wrong and how sure it sounds. You'll trust its tone less from then on, which is the point.
  • Add one line to your prompts. "If you're not sure, say so, and tell me where you found each fact."
  • Check the specifics. Every name, number, quote and link in anything you'll send or rely on.
For your team
  • Run the twenty-question test. Write twenty questions from your own work where you know the answers. Run them through the tools you use. Count the confident wrong ones. That's your real starting point, in an hour.
  • Show a real example. Share one confident, wrong AI answer from your own field at the next team meeting. Nothing teaches checking faster.
  • Mark the riskier tasks. List where your team uses AI for citations, numbers, niche facts or summaries, and agree how each gets checked.
