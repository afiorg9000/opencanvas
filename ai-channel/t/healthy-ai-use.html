Am I leaning on AI too much?

Habits, boundaries and knowing when to disconnect. For you, and for the team you manage.

About 9 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

Probably not, if AI is saving you time on work you could still do yourself and you check what it gives you. The warning signs are different: you can't start without it, you've stopped checking, or it has become the main place you take how you feel.

Healthy use is less about hours and more about three habits: think first, check after, and keep people in the loop.

1 · The test

What healthy use looks like

Think of the difference between a calculator and a crutch. A calculator makes you faster at something you understand. A crutch takes weight you can no longer carry. Both are useful. The question is which one AI has become for you, task by task.

A simple test: if the tool disappeared tomorrow, could you still do the work, just slower? If yes, it's a calculator. If you'd be stuck, that's not automatically a problem (few of us can do long division any more), but it should be a choice you made, not one that happened to you.

Think first

You bring the question

You know roughly what good looks like before you ask, so you can tell when the answer is off.

Check after

You stay the editor

Facts, figures and names get checked before anything leaves your hands. Your name is on it, not the tool's.

People in the loop

It doesn't replace people

Hard calls, feedback and how you feel still go to a person at some point. AI can help you prepare; it shouldn't be the only voice.

2 · Evidence

What the research says so far

Early, mostly short-term, and worth taking seriously anyway.

Nobody has a long-term study of what years of daily AI use do to how we think or feel. The tools are too new. What we have are surveys, small experiments and a few larger trials, and they point in the same direction often enough to act on.

Fairly clear

Trusting the tool more means checking less

In a survey of 319 knowledge workers describing 936 real tasks, researchers at Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon found that the more people trusted AI, the less critical thinking they reported putting in. People who were confident in their own skills checked more.1

Fairly clear

Heavy use and loneliness travel together

In a four-week trial with 981 people run by the MIT Media Lab and OpenAI, those who used a chatbot more each day reported more loneliness, more emotional dependence on it and less time socializing with people.2 Emotionally heavy use was concentrated in a small group of very heavy users.3

Still uncertain

Does AI cause it, or attract it?

The loneliness finding is a link, not proof of cause. Lonely people may simply turn to chatbots more. The same trial found that the type of chatbot people were assigned made no significant difference; how much they chose to use it did.2

Still uncertain

"Cognitive debt"

A small MIT study of 54 people writing essays found that those using ChatGPT showed weaker brain connectivity and struggled to quote their own essays.4 It's a preprint with a small sample. Treat it as a reason to keep some thinking for yourself, not as a verdict.

The practical reading: the risk isn't the tool, it's the pattern. Occasional, deliberate use looks fine on the evidence so far. Constant, unchecked or emotionally load-bearing use is where the warning signs cluster.

3 · Self-check

Seven signs it's tipping

Two or three of these is a nudge. Most of them is a conversation worth having with yourself.

  • You can't start without it. A blank page feels impossible until you've asked the tool.
  • You've stopped checking. Figures and names go straight into your work because "it's usually right."
  • You accept answers you'd question from a colleague. The confident tone does the convincing.
  • Sessions run long and late. One question turns into an hour, often at night.
  • It's the first place you go when you're upset. Before a friend, a partner or anyone who knows you.
  • You'd rather people didn't know how much you use it. At work or at home.
  • A skill you used to have feels rusty. Writing a tricky email, doing the sums, reading a long document.
4 · Habits

Habits that keep AI a tool

  1. Take five minutes before you ask. Jot down what you think the answer is, or the outline you'd write. Then compare. You'll spot weak answers faster, and you keep the muscle.
  2. Check the specifics, every time. Facts, figures, names, quotes and links. If it matters, open the source yourself. (Why this matters: why AI sounds so confident when it's wrong.)
  3. Keep one skill AI-free. Pick something you want to stay good at, such as first drafts, analysis or feedback, and do it yourself at least some of the time.
  4. Set a stop point. Decide what "done" looks like before you start a session. Long, open-ended chats drift. Some tools now nudge you to take a break during long sessions; ChatGPT added one in 2025.5 Don't wait for the nudge.
  5. Keep big personal decisions for people. Use AI to list options or prepare for a conversation. Make the call with someone who knows you and will still be there next week.
  6. Notice the time of day. Late-night use is where loops and low moods tend to meet. If you're reaching for a chatbot at 1am, that's useful information about the day you've had.
5 · Boundaries

Knowing when to disconnect

Plenty of people talk to AI about how they feel, and some find it helps them put things into words. That's fine as one input among several. It becomes a problem when it's the only one.

The American Psychological Association's 2025 health advisory is plain about it: general chatbots and wellness apps "should not be used as a replacement for a qualified mental health care provider," and "relying solely on an app during a mental health emergency can be dangerous."6 Chatbots also tend to agree with whoever they're talking to, which is the opposite of what you need when your thinking is stuck in a loop.

Step away and talk to a person when:

  • you're using AI to get through the day emotionally, most days;
  • it's the only "one" you're telling about something that's weighing on you;
  • you feel worse, more anxious or more alone after long sessions;
  • you're asking it about harming yourself, or about a crisis.

If you're struggling, talk to a person

A friend, family member, your doctor, or a counselor or therapist. If your workplace has an employee assistance program, it's confidential and usually free. If you're in crisis or worried about your safety:

  • Anywhere: call your local emergency number if you or someone else is in immediate danger.
  • United States: call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), 24/7.7
  • UK and Ireland: call Samaritans free on 116 123, day or night.8
  • Elsewhere: findahelpline.com lists free, confidential helplines in more than 175 countries.9

This guide is general information about habits, not medical or mental health advice.

6 · For managers

If you manage a team: the norms to set

Most unhealthy AI use at work starts as a workload problem or a mixed message.

Teams get mixed messages about AI all the time: "use it more" from one meeting, "don't trust it" from the next. People fill the gap on their own, and often quietly. The result shows up in the work. In a September 2025 survey of US desk workers by BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab, 40% said they'd received "workslop" in the past month: AI-generated work that looks finished but isn't, and lands on someone else to fix.10

Five norms do most of the work. Say them out loud, then write them down.

Say when AI helped
No stigma, no badge of honor. A line like "first draft with AI, figures checked by me" lets reviewers know what to look at, and stops hidden use.
The sender checks, not the receiver
Whoever sends the work owns it. Passing on unchecked AI output is passing on work, and that's what makes workslop expensive.
Don't reward volume
Measure outcomes, not how many prompts people run or how much they produce. Counting usage tells people to use it more, whether it helps or not.
Protect the learning tasks
Newer staff learn by doing the first draft, the analysis, the hard email. Let them do some of it unaided first, then compare with AI. Otherwise you'll have people who can prompt but can't judge.
Keep the evenings
AI makes it easy to produce at any hour. Make it clear that faster tools don't mean longer days, and watch for people using AI to keep up with a load that's simply too big. That's a workload conversation, not a tool problem.

If someone is struggling. You're not their therapist, and you don't need to be. Ask how they are, listen, and point them to your employee assistance program, their doctor or the helplines above. If you're worried about their immediate safety, act on it.

7 · Actions

What you can do

For you
  • Run the tool-free hour. Pick one task you now always do with AI. Do it once this week without it, clock running. Notice what was easy, what was hard and what you'd forgotten.
  • Choose your one AI-free skill. Write it down. Keep doing it yourself at least part of the time.
  • Set a stop rule. For example: no chatbot after 10pm, or sessions end when the task is done, not when the conversation runs out.
  • Name your person. Who do you talk to when something's weighing on you? If the honest answer is "the chatbot," call someone this week.
For your team
  • Write the five norms on one page. Say when AI helped; the sender checks; outcomes not volume; protect learning tasks; keep the evenings.
  • Run a lunch-hour show-and-tell. Everyone brings one task where AI helped and one where it got in the way. You'll learn more than any usage report tells you.
  • Ask about load, not just tools. In one-to-ones, ask what people use AI for. If the answer is "to keep up," look at the workload.
  • Put support where people can find it. Add your employee assistance program and local helplines to the same page as your AI rules.
