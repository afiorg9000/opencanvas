Is our AI an asset or a liability?

AI deployment without responsible AI is a liability, not an asset. The losses, the court cases and the regulation behind that sentence, and the five questions a board should ask this quarter.

About 8 minutesLast checked November 2026

The short answer

It depends almost entirely on the controls around it. Large companies already report real money lost to AI risks, and courts have made clear that "the AI did it" isn't a defense.

The organizations that build governance in from the start (an owner, monitoring, tests, a stop button) report better results than those that bolt it on later. With EU rules phasing in and buyers asking AI assistants which suppliers to trust, being able to show your controls is now part of the asset.

Read these with care. The EY figures come from a survey of executives at companies with annual revenue above US$1 billion, so they're self-reported estimates, not audited accounts.2 The most common risks those executives named were non-compliance with AI regulations (57%), damage to sustainability goals (55%) and biased outputs (53%).1 Smaller firms will see smaller numbers, but the pattern holds: losses come from gaps in control, not from AI as such.

In the UK edition of the same survey, only 17% of C-suite respondents could correctly identify the right controls for a set of AI risks.2 That's the gap a board can close.

"The AI did it" isn't a defense

Two cases every executive should know.

  • Air Canada, 2024. The airline's website chatbot told a grieving customer he could claim a bereavement fare after booking. The policy said otherwise. Air Canada argued, in effect, that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions." The British Columbia tribunal disagreed: "It should be obvious to Air Canada that it is responsible for all the information on its website." It ordered the airline to pay the difference.5
  • Deloitte Australia, 2025. A report for a federal department, worth about AU$440,000, was found to contain references that didn't exist and a made-up quote from a court judgment. A corrected version disclosed that a generative AI tool had been used, and Deloitte agreed to repay the final instalment of the contract.6

Neither case involved new AI law. Ordinary rules on misrepresentation and on delivering what a contract promises were enough. Your existing duties (consumer, employment, data protection, professional) already apply to anything your AI says or does in your name.

Built in beats bolted on

EY found that companies with real-time monitoring of their AI were 34% more likely to report improved revenue growth and 65% more likely to report improved cost savings.1 That's a correlation from a survey, not proof that monitoring causes growth. Better-run companies may simply do both. But it cuts against the idea that governance is a brake.

The practical argument is stronger. Retrofitting is expensive because the evidence you need later has to be collected from day one: logs you didn't keep can't be recreated, tests you didn't run can't be replayed against last year's model, and an owner nobody named can't explain a decision. The controls that matter are the same ones the main frameworks ask for: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and the ISO/IEC 42001 management-system standard both start with ownership and an inventory.1112

Regulation is arriving on a timetable

The EU AI Act applies to organizations in the EU, and to those outside it whose AI output is used in the EU.7 Its banned practices and AI-literacy duty have applied since February 2025, and its transparency duties since August 2026. In July 2026 the EU pushed the main high-risk obligations back, to 2 December 2027 for areas like hiring, credit and education, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into regulated products.8

That's a delay, not a repeal. Fines for most breaches reach EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher, and EUR 35 million or 7% for banned practices.7 A system you approve this year will still be running when the high-risk rules apply. The EU AI Act risk tier checker tells you where a given system lands.

AI answer engines are deciding who gets seen

Buyers are changing where they look. In a March 2026 survey of 1,076 B2B software buyers by the review platform G2, 51% said they now start research with an AI chatbot more often than with Google, up from 29% in G2's 2025 survey.9 And when Google shows an AI summary, people click through to a website less: in Pew Research Center's tracking of 900 US adults, users clicked a regular result on 8% of visits with a summary, against 15% without one.10

Here's what that means for responsible AI, and it's our reading rather than a measured effect. When a buyer asks an assistant which suppliers handle data carefully, the answer is built from what's public. A specific, published AI-use policy, named controls and a clean record are things it can find. Good intentions on an internal wiki aren't. Governance you can show is starting to work like reputation.

Five questions for the next board meeting

  1. What AI are we running, and who owns each one? Ask for the inventory. If it takes more than a week to produce, that's the first finding.
  2. Which of these could hurt a customer, an employee or our license to operate? Those need tests, logs and a named human reviewer before anything else.
  3. How would we know if it went wrong? Monitoring, an incident route, and a way to switch it off in minutes.
  4. What are our people using that we never approved? Shadow AI is where most of the uncontrolled data exposure sits.
  5. Could we show a customer or regulator our controls tomorrow? If the answer is a slide, not evidence, there's work to do.

What you can do

For the leadership team

  1. Name one accountable executive. Not a committee. One person who reports on AI risk to the board each quarter.
  2. Fund the boring parts. Inventory, monitoring and testing come before the next pilot, not after it.
  3. Publish what you do. A short, specific public page on how you use AI and protect data. It answers customers, regulators and the AI assistants buyers now ask.
  4. Hold the 90-day review. Put the five questions on the agenda now and again in three months. Track how the answers change.
